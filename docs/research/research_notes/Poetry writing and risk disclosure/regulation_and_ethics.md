# Regulation and ethics of software that claims to detect suicide or violence risk from text (for PoesIA)

Scope: what changes legally (EU MDR, Swiss MedDO, EU AI Act, GDPR) and ethically if PoesIA said it
"detects" or "screens for" suicide/harm risk or intent, compared with offering a safety net that
shows helplines. State of the law as checked on 2026-10-08. **This is not legal advice.** Legal
texts were downloaded from EUR-Lex / ec.europa.eu / fedlex and grepped; quotes below are verbatim
from those downloads. "Not seen" marks a source whose text was not opened.

Context checked in the repo (not a source, for the writer): PoesIA's safety screen
(module `poesia.safety`, tests in `tests/test_safety_screen.py`) uses phrase lists per language plus
a question to the language model, and opens a pause with helplines (es/en/it, names checked on
143.ch/147.ch per `docs/hack_apertus/PACKAGING_UX_PLAN.md` U9). So it is an AI-assisted text
classifier whose only output is showing resources.

## 1. EU MDR 2017/745 and MDCG 2019-11: would "detects suicide risk" make PoesIA a medical device, and which class?

### Takeaway
Qualification turns on the manufacturer's stated **intended purpose**, which includes promotional
statements: claiming PoesIA "detects", "screens for" or "predicts" suicide risk states a medical
purpose ("diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, prediction ... of disease"), which would very likely
make the screen medical device software, classified under Rule 11 at class IIa at minimum and most
plausibly IIb or III (MDCG's own example puts a depression-scoring app at IIb; a suicide-risk
decision can lead to death). A pause that only offers helplines, framed as a non-medical safety
courtesy in a creative-writing tool, fits the "wellness" / non-medical side, but only if the words
used everywhere (README, report, UI, demo video) avoid medical-purpose claims.

### Cited Findings
- MDR Art. 2(1), verbatim: "‘medical device’ means any instrument, apparatus, appliance, software, implant, reagent, material or other article intended by the manufacturer to be used, alone or in combination, for human beings for one or more of the following specific medical purposes: — diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, prediction, prognosis, treatment or alleviation of disease, — diagnosis, monitoring, treatment, alleviation of, or compensation for, an injury or disability, — investigation, replacement or modification of the anatomy or of a physiological or pathological process or state, — providing information by means of in vitro examination of specimens ..." — [Regulation (EU) 2017/745, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0745)
- MDR Art. 2(12), verbatim: "‘intended purpose’ means the use for which a device is intended according to the data supplied by the manufacturer on the label, in the instructions for use or in promotional or sales materials or statements and as specified by the manufacturer in the clinical evaluation;" — [MDR, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0745)
- MDR Recital 19, verbatim: "software in its own right, when specifically intended by the manufacturer to be used for one or more of the medical purposes set out in the definition of a medical device, qualifies as a medical device, while software for general purposes, even when used in a healthcare setting, or software intended for life-style and well-being purposes is not a medical device." — [MDR, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0745)
- MDR Annex VIII, Rule 11 (section 6.3), verbatim: "Software intended to provide information which is used to take decisions with diagnosis or therapeutic purposes is classified as class IIa, except if such decisions have an impact that may cause: — death or an irreversible deterioration of a person's state of health, in which case it is in class III; or — a serious deterioration of a person's state of health or a surgical intervention, in which case it is classified as class IIb. Software intended to monitor physiological processes is classified as class IIa, except if ... All other software is classified as class I." — [MDR Annex VIII, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0745)
- MDR Art. 2(27) and (28): "‘making available on the market’ means any supply of a device ... for distribution, consumption or use on the Union market in the course of a commercial activity, whether in return for payment or free of charge"; "‘placing on the market’ means the first making available of a device ... on the Union market". Art. 2(30): "‘manufacturer’ means a natural or legal person who manufactures ... a device ... and markets that device under its name or trademark". — [MDR, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0745)
- MDCG 2019-11 **Rev.1 (June 2025)** is the current revision (original October 2019). Its change list includes "Addition of considerations related to the importance of crafting a clear intended purpose", "Addition of new examples, including MDSW intended to treat", and "Addition of clarification to Rule 11 (Subrule a). Addition of references and examples on devices intended to prevent the risk of illness". It states it is "not legally binding and only the Court of Justice of the European Union can give binding interpretations of Union law." — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1 PDF, European Commission](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf); release note [EC, 17 June 2025](https://health.ec.europa.eu/latest-updates/update-mdcg-2019-11-rev1-qualification-and-classification-software-regulation-eu-2017745-and-2025-06-17_en)
- MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, p. 9, verbatim: "software only intended for non-medical purposes ... such as invoicing, staff planning, e-mailing, web or voice messaging, data parsing, word processing, and back-up, wellness or fitness apps, do not qualify as MDSW." Same page: "the risk of harm to patients, users of the software, or any other person, related to the use of the software within healthcare, including a possible malfunction is not a criterion on whether the software qualifies as a medical device." — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- MDCG decision steps (pp. 12–13), verbatim: step 3 "if the software does perform an action on data, or performs an action beyond storage, archival, communication, simple search, lossless compression ... then it may be a MDSW"; step 4 "is the action for the benefit of individual patients?"; step 5 "Is the software medical device software (MDSW) according to the definition of this guidance?" — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- MDCG examples of qualifying mental-health MDSW (section 3.2): "MDSW intended to assess, monitor, and manage depression. The MDSW provides patients with questionnaires to track their mood, symptoms, and activities..."; and MDSW "intended to alleviate certain eating disorder behaviours such as bulimia and anorexia" (p. 9). Note 4: "MDSW may be intended to be used by healthcare professionals or laypersons". — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- MDCG on Rule 11(a), pp. 17–18, verbatim: the wording "describes, in very general terms, the ‘mode of action’ which is characteristic of all MDSW. Therefore, this sub-rule is generally applicable to all MDSW"; and (new in Rev.1) "a device intended to prevent the risk of illnesses or pathologies by analysing physiological parameters ... can be considered as a device providing information which is used to take decisions with diagnosis purpose (potential detection of pathologies) and in this case is in class IIa." Higher classes apply "where such decisions, if based on incorrect information from the MDSW, are reasonably likely to have an impact that may cause: i. death or an irreversible deterioration ... class III; ii. serious deterioration ... class IIb." — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- MDCG classification example, p. 34, verbatim: "Diagnostic MDSW intended for scoring depression based on inputted data on a patient’s symptoms (e.g. mood, anxiety) should be classified as class IIb under Rule 11(a), (IMDRF Risk Category III.ii) as the healthcare situation (depression) is serious and the significance of the information is ‘diagnosis’)." Same page: "Any change to the intended purpose or the healthcare context/situation in which that same device is used might result in a different risk class." — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Detects suicide risk / intent", "screens for risk", "predicts crisis" each map onto Art. 2(1) "diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, prediction" of a disease/condition, for an individual (decision step 4), by acting on data (step 3). Because Art. 2(12) counts "promotional ... materials or statements", a hackathon report, README or demo video making that claim can itself establish the intended purpose. The phrasing is the decisive variable.
- Class if qualified: no MDCG example names suicide risk. By analogy with the depression-scoring example (IIb), and because a missed or wrong suicide-risk output is plausibly "reasonably likely" to contribute to death, a regulator could argue class III. IIb–III is my inference, not a stated rule; at minimum IIa. Every class from IIa up needs a notified body, so this would be well beyond a hackathon project.
- A pause that only shows helplines and never claims to assess the person: arguably a general-purpose / well-being feature of a word-processing-like tool (p. 9 lists "word processing" and "wellness" apps as non-MDSW). It still "acts on data" (step 3), so the argument rests on the absence of a medical intended purpose, not on what the code does. Wording such as "if a text sounds like you may be in pain, here are people to talk to; this is not an assessment" stays on the safe side. Wording such as "PoesIA detects when users are at risk" does not.
- MDCG p. 9 says risk of harm "is not a criterion" for qualification. A safety feature is therefore not a medical device just because it relates to life and death; what matters is the claim.
- Free, open-source release: the MDR "making available" definition covers supply "free of charge" but "in the course of a commercial activity". Whether a non-commercial hobby release on GitHub is a "commercial activity" is unsettled (see Gaps). Do not rely on this as a defence if a medical claim is made.

### Gaps
- No MDCG/MDR example covers suicide-risk or violence-risk detection from free text. The class (IIb vs III) is inference.
- Not checked: the EU Blue Guide (2022) on what counts as "commercial activity" for free/open-source products, and whether the Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853 (software as product; free and open-source software outside commercial activity excluded, as I recall) applies. Not seen.
- Not checked: MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1 annex/flowchart figure (Figure 1) beyond the text extracted.

## 2. Switzerland: MedDO alignment with the MDR

### Takeaway
The Swiss Medical Devices Ordinance (MedDO, SR 812.213) copies the MDR's medical-purpose list into
its own Art. 3 and classifies devices by reference to MDR Annex VIII, so the same "detects risk"
analysis and the same Rule 11 classes would apply in Switzerland.

### Cited Findings
- MedDO Art. 3(1), English version in force 1 July 2026, verbatim: "Medical devices are instruments, apparatus, appliances, softwares, implants, reagents, materials or other articles: a. that are intended by their manufacturer to be used for human beings; b. that do not achieve their principal intended action ... by pharmacological, immunological or metabolic means ...; and c. that serve to fulfil one or more of the following specific medical purposes either alone or in combination: 1. diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, prediction, prognosis, treatment or alleviation of disease, 2. diagnosis, monitoring, treatment, alleviation of, or compensation for, injuries or disabilities, ..." — [MedDO SR 812.213, fedlex (version 20260701, en)](https://fedlex.data.admin.ch/filestore/fedlex.data.admin.ch/eli/cc/2020/552/20260701/en/html/fedlex-data-admin-ch-eli-cc-2020-552-20260701-en-html.html)
- MedDO Art. 15, verbatim: "Devices shall be divided into classes I, IIa, IIb and III, taking into account the intended purpose of the devices and their inherent risks. This classification must comply with the provisions of Annex VIII to EU-MDR, taking account of the implementing acts of the European Commission listed in Annex 5a." — [MedDO, fedlex](https://fedlex.data.admin.ch/filestore/fedlex.data.admin.ch/eli/cc/2020/552/20260701/en/html/fedlex-data-admin-ch-eli-cc-2020-552-20260701-en-html.html)
- MedDO Art. 16(2): product information "must be written in all three official languages of Switzerland." — [MedDO, fedlex](https://fedlex.data.admin.ch/filestore/fedlex.data.admin.ch/eli/cc/2020/552/20260701/en/html/fedlex-data-admin-ch-eli-cc-2020-552-20260701-en-html.html)

### Inferences
- Because Rule 11 applies through MedDO Art. 15, a "detects suicide risk" claim would land in the same class in Switzerland as in the EU. A Swiss hackathon does not shelter the claim.
- English texts on fedlex are translations without legal force (only German, French and Italian are authentic); quote the English for readability, but flag this in the report.

### Gaps
- Not checked: Swissmedic's guidance on standalone medical device software (an older information sheet MU101_30_008d appears in search results only through a third-party mirror; not seen on swissmedic.ch).
- Not checked: the status of the EU–Swiss Mutual Recognition Agreement for medical devices (EU treats Switzerland as a third country since May 2021, as I recall; not verified here), and Switzerland's own AI regulation plans (no Swiss equivalent of the AI Act was found or checked).
- Swiss data protection (revised FADP, sensitive data incl. health) not checked.

## 3. EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689, as amended by the Digital Omnibus on AI 2026/1744): high-risk, emotion recognition, transparency

### Takeaway
A text-based risk screen is **not** an "emotion recognition system" under the AI Act, because that
definition requires biometric data and the Commission's guidelines say emotion inference from
written text is not biometric; so Art. 5(1)(f), Annex III 1(c) and Art. 50(3) do not bite. The
high-risk route is Art. 6(1): if a "detects suicide risk" claim made the screen a MDR device of
class IIa or higher (needing a notified body), it would also be a high-risk AI system, with
obligations applying from **2 August 2028** after the Digital Omnibus. The safety-net framing
keeps PoesIA out of high-risk; its generative side still meets Art. 50 transparency duties
(applying since 2 August 2026), from which the open-source exemption does not shield it.

### Cited Findings
- Art. 3(39), verbatim: "‘emotion recognition system’ means an AI system for the purpose of identifying or inferring emotions or intentions of natural persons on the basis of their biometric data". Art. 3(34): "‘biometric data’ means personal data resulting from specific technical processing relating to the physical, physiological or behavioural characteristics of a natural person, such as facial images or dactyloscopic data". — [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689)
- Recital 18 lists "emotions or intentions such as happiness, sadness, anger, surprise, disgust, embarrassment, excitement, shame, contempt, satisfaction and amusement. It does not include physical states, such as pain or fatigue". — [AI Act, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689)
- Commission Guidelines on prohibited AI practices, C(2025) 5052 final (29.7.2025), PDF p. 85, verbatim: "An AI system inferring emotions from written text (content/sentiment analyses) to define the style or the tone of a certain article is not based on biometric data and therefore does not fall within the scope of the prohibition." and "An AI system inferring emotions from key stroke (way of typing), facial expressions, body postures or movements is based on biometric data and falls within the scope of the prohibition." — [Commission Guidelines C(2025) 5052](https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/dae/redirection/document/112367); [EC page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/commission-publishes-guidelines-prohibited-artificial-intelligence-ai-practices-defined-ai-act)
- Art. 5(1)(f), verbatim, prohibits "the use of AI systems to infer emotions of a natural person in the areas of workplace and education institutions, except where the use of the AI system is intended to be put in place or into the market for medical or safety reasons". — [AI Act, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689)
- Annex III point 1(c) (high-risk, biometrics): "AI systems intended to be used for emotion recognition." — [AI Act, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689)
- Art. 6(1), verbatim: an AI system "shall be considered to be high-risk where both of the following conditions are fulfilled: (a) the AI system is intended to be used as a safety component of a product, or the AI system is itself a product, covered by the Union harmonisation legislation listed in Annex I; (b) the product ... is required to undergo a third-party conformity assessment". Annex I Section A point 11 lists Regulation (EU) 2017/745 (MDR). — [AI Act, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689)
- Art. 3(12): "‘intended purpose’ means the use for which an AI system is intended by the provider, including the specific context and conditions of use, as specified in the information supplied by the provider in the instructions for use, promotional or sales materials and statements, as well as in the technical documentation". — [AI Act, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689)
- Art. 3(3): "‘provider’ means a natural or legal person ... that develops an AI system ... and places it on the market or puts the AI system into service under its own name or trademark, whether for payment or free of charge". Art. 3(10): "‘making available on the market’ means the supply of an AI system ... in the course of a commercial activity, whether in return for payment or free of charge". — [AI Act, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689)
- Art. 2(12), verbatim: "This Regulation does not apply to AI systems released under free and open-source licences, unless they are placed on the market or put into service as high-risk AI systems or as an AI system that falls under Article 5 or 50." Art. 2(10): "This Regulation does not apply to obligations of deployers who are natural persons using AI systems in the course of a purely personal non-professional activity." — [AI Act, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689)
- Art. 50(1), verbatim: "Providers shall ensure that AI systems intended to interact directly with natural persons are designed and developed in such a way that the natural persons concerned are informed that they are interacting with an AI system, unless this is obvious from the point of view of a natural person who is reasonably well-informed, observant and circumspect". Art. 50(2): providers of systems "generating synthetic audio, image, video or text content, shall ensure that the outputs of the AI system are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated ... This obligation shall not apply to the extent the AI systems perform an assistive function for standard editing or do not substantially alter the input data provided by the deployer or the semantics thereof". Art. 50(3): "Deployers of an emotion recognition system or a biometric categorisation system shall inform the natural persons exposed thereto of the operation of the system". — [AI Act, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689)
- Original application dates (Art. 113): general application 2 August 2026; Chapters I–II (incl. Art. 5 prohibitions) from 2 February 2025; "Article 6(1) and the corresponding obligations in this Regulation shall apply from 2 August 2027." — [AI Act, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689)
- **Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 of 8 July 2026** amends Art. 113: "(c) Chapter III, Sections 1, 2, and 3, with the exception of Article 6(5), shall apply from: (i) 2 December 2027 as regards AI systems classified as high-risk pursuant to Article 6(2) and Annex III; and (ii) 2 August 2028 as regards AI systems classified as high-risk pursuant to Article 6(1) and Annex I". It also adds a four-month transition for Art. 50(2) for systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 (to 2 December 2026), and new Art. 5(1)(ba)/(bb) prohibitions applying from 2 December 2026 (on generation/manipulation of intimate imagery, not relevant here). — [Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32026R1744). Secondary reports give entry into force as 27 July 2026 after OJ publication on 24 July 2026 — [aiacto.eu](https://www.aiacto.eu/en/blog/digital-omnibus-ai-act-report-2027) (the EUR-Lex text itself adds "Articles 102 to 110 shall apply from 27 July 2026", consistent with that).

### Inferences
- Emotion recognition: PoesIA reads typed text only (not keystroke timing, voice or face), so it is outside Art. 3(39), and therefore outside Art. 5(1)(f) (which in any case only covers workplace/education and exempts "medical or safety reasons"), Annex III 1(c) and Art. 50(3). If a future version used typing rhythm or voice recitation (the `armonia` recitation module) to infer distress, that would become biometric-based emotion recognition and change the analysis.
- "Intentions" sit inside the emotion-recognition definition. A claim to detect "intent" to self-harm or to harm others moves the vocabulary toward Art. 3(39), but without biometric input it still falls outside. The stronger effect of "intent" language is under the MDR (prediction) and, for violence, toward law-enforcement-like uses (Annex III point 6, not checked in detail).
- High-risk: only via Art. 6(1) + MDR. If the MDR claim is avoided (Q1), PoesIA is not high-risk. If the claim were made, a class IIa+ MDSW needs a notified body (third-party conformity assessment), so Art. 6(1)(b) is met and the AI system is high-risk. For Annex I products those obligations apply from 2 August 2028 (post-Omnibus); the AI Act's provider definitions cover free supply.
- Open-source exemption (Art. 2(12)) does not remove Art. 50: PoesIA generates text, so Art. 50(2) marking may apply, unless the "assistive function for standard editing" carve-out fits (it partly does: PoesIA helps a writer write their own lines). Art. 50(1) is likely met already if the UI says it is an AI tool. Whether a local-only GitHub release is "placed on the market ... in the course of a commercial activity" is uncertain.
- A single person using PoesIA privately is outside deployer duties (Art. 2(10)); duties fall on the provider (the developer) only if PoesIA is placed on the market or put into service.

### Gaps
- No Commission guidance found specifically on mental-health or suicide-risk text classifiers under the AI Act.
- Not checked: Commission guidelines on the high-risk classification under Art. 6 (if published) and the Omnibus's new Art. 6(1a) ("non-safety related aspects of user assistance ...") in full; its effect on a safety pause was not analysed.
- Not checked: whether the Commission guidelines paragraph number containing the "written text" example is (251); the PDF page (85) is certain.

## 4. GDPR Art. 9: health data inferred from text, and whether local-only processing changes the developer's duties

### Takeaway
A screen that infers suicide risk from a poem processes data that can "reveal" mental health,
which the CJEU treats as special-category data even when the sensitive fact is only deduced. But if
PoesIA runs only on the user's machine and nothing reaches the developer, the developer is very
likely not a controller (does not process the data), and the user writing their own poems is in
the household exemption. The GDPR then mainly reaches the developer as a design expectation
(Recital 78), and would bite fully if any server, telemetry, logging or reporting to third parties
were added.

### Cited Findings
- Art. 9(1), verbatim: "Processing of ... data concerning health ... shall be prohibited." Exceptions include Art. 9(2)(a) "the data subject has given explicit consent ..." and 9(2)(c) "processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person where the data subject is physically or legally incapable of giving consent". — [Regulation (EU) 2016/679, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679)
- Art. 4(15): "‘data concerning health’ means personal data related to the physical or mental health of a natural person, including the provision of health care services, which reveal information about his or her health status". Recital 35: health data "should include all data pertaining to the health status of a data subject which reveal information relating to the past, current or future physical or mental health status". — [GDPR, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679)
- CJEU (Grand Chamber), C-184/20 *OT v Vyriausioji tarnybinės etikos komisija*, 1 August 2022, para. 123: the verb ‘reveal’ "is consistent with the taking into account of processing not only of inherently sensitive data, but also of data revealing information of that nature indirectly, following an intellectual operation involving deduction or cross-referencing"; para. 125: "a wide interpretation of the terms ‘special categories of personal data’ and ‘sensitive data’ is confirmed by the objective of ... the GDPR". The ruling: data "liable to disclose indirectly the sexual orientation of a natural person constitutes processing of special categories of personal data". — [C-184/20, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:62020CJ0184)
- Art. 4(7): "‘controller’ means the natural or legal person ... which, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing of personal data". — [GDPR, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679)
- Art. 2(2)(c) and Recital 18, verbatim: the GDPR does not apply to processing "by a natural person in the course of a purely personal or household activity"; "However, this Regulation applies to controllers or processors which provide the means for processing personal data for such personal or household activities." — [GDPR, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679)
- Recital 78, verbatim: "producers of the products, services and applications should be encouraged to take into account the right to data protection when developing and designing such products, services and applications and, with due regard to the state of the art, to make sure that controllers and processors are able to fulfil their data protection obligations." — [GDPR, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679)

### Inferences
- An inferred "at risk" flag about an identifiable writer is health data under Art. 4(15) read with C-184/20 (the court's reasoning on "reveal" covers deductions; it was about sexual orientation but para. 124 expressly applies the same reading to "data concerning health").
- Local-only: if the developer never receives text, flags or telemetry, the developer does not process personal data and is not a controller. Recital 18's "provide the means" sentence is the risk: it is usually read as covering service providers who do process (e.g. a hosted platform), not authors of software that runs entirely offline. That reading is the common view but is my inference; I found no CJEU or EDPB ruling on purely local software.
- What would change the position: any hosted version (e.g. the Hack Apertus demo calling a remote Apertus endpoint with the user's text), crash logs or analytics containing text, saving flags, or a feature that alerts a third party. Then the operator becomes a controller processing Art. 9 data and needs an Art. 9(2) exception (explicit consent being the realistic one); Art. 9(2)(c) vital interests only covers someone "physically or legally incapable of giving consent". The Celedonia et al. reading (Q5) of why Facebook's tool was kept out of the EU rests on this.
- "Detects risk" vs "safety net" matters less for GDPR than for the MDR: the processing (classifying text for risk) is the same either way. What GDPR cares about is where the data goes and whether a flag is stored. The safety-net design (no storage, no reporting, the person decides) is also the GDPR-minimal design.
- For a public hackathon demo hosted anywhere (e.g. a Hugging Face Space or the CSCS endpoint), text leaves the machine; the "nothing leaves the machine" claim then holds only for the local build. The report should say which build the claim covers.

### Gaps
- No EDPB guidance checked on controller status of developers of on-device software or on inferred mental-health data in apps (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020 on controller/processor not opened).
- Netherlands-specific implementation (UAVG) not checked; for a Dutch developer the GDPR applies directly, but national rules on health data processing (UAVG Art. 30) were not opened.

## 5. Ethics of automated suicide/violence-risk detection outside clinical care

### Takeaway
The scholarly critiques of platform suicide detection (Facebook's 2017+ program) converge on:
no consent or awareness, no transparency or external review, false positives leading to police
"wellness checks" and involuntary treatment, stigma, and the fact that population-level
prediction of suicide is near useless at the individual level (positive predictive value ≤1% in
most clinical models). Those harms come from **detection that triggers action by others**. A tool
that never stores, never reports, and only offers the writer resources avoids almost all of them.
"Detects risk" language would invite the same critiques and promise an accuracy no model has.

### Cited Findings
- Marks: AI suicide prediction runs on two tracks; "social suicide prediction typically occurs outside the healthcare system where it is almost completely unregulated. Corporations maintain their suicide prediction methods as proprietary trade secrets. Despite this lack of transparency, social suicide predictions are deployed globally to affect people’s lives every day. Yet little is known about their safety or effectiveness." (abstract, p. 98) — [Marks 2019, Yale J. Law & Tech. (PDF)](https://yjolt.org/sites/default/files/21_yale_j.l._tech._special_issue_98.pdf)
- Marks, p. 99: the risks "include stigmatization of people with mental illness, the transfer of sensitive personal data to third-parties such as advertisers and data brokers, unnecessary involuntary confinement, violent confrontations with police, exacerbation of mental health conditions, and paradoxical increases in suicide risk." p. 111: "autonomy risks include censorship, unnecessary confinement or civil commitment, and in countries where suicide attempts are illegal, criminal penalties including fines and incarceration." — [Marks 2019](https://yjolt.org/sites/default/files/21_yale_j.l._tech._special_issue_98.pdf)
- Marks, p. 108: Facebook announced its program "had initiated over 100 such ‘wellness checks’" and that it would expand globally in "most of the countries in which it operates, with the exception of those in the European Union (EU)." p. 109: Facebook "conducted at least 3,500 wellness checks in the U.S. and abroad"; review by its ethics board "occurs at the company’s discretion". — [Marks 2019](https://yjolt.org/sites/default/files/21_yale_j.l._tech._special_issue_98.pdf)
- Marks, p. 120 (recommendations): "suicide prediction methods could be made more transparent, and users could be given unambiguous opportunities to opt-out and delete prediction information". Marks also notes even medical prediction models "still produce a significant number of false positives and false negatives. As a result, they are primarily used for research and not to guide clinical decision-making." (p. 107) — [Marks 2019](https://yjolt.org/sites/default/files/21_yale_j.l._tech._special_issue_98.pdf)
- Celedonia et al. 2021: "Users of Facebook, in general, appear to be unaware of the platform’s suicide risk detection strategy." They describe the EU absence as "the main reason why Facebook’s suicide algorithm is banned in the European Union (EU). Stricter rules due to the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) requires users to provide websites specific consent to collect sensitive data such as that pertaining to someone’s mental health." — [Celedonia et al. 2021, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8284882/). Caution: "banned" is the authors' word; Marks (above) reports Facebook's own decision to exclude the EU. I found no regulatory decision that banned it.
- Celedonia et al.: false positives "can lead to numerous undesirable outcomes, included stigmatization, unnecessary psychiatric inpatient hospitalizations, trauma from stressful and potentially violent encounters with law enforcement, and exacerbation of mental illness symptoms and suicide risk"; the algorithm was trained "to detect key words and phrases related to suicide risk in English, Spanish, Portuguese, and Arabic" and experts question whether it "works equally when applied to different ethnic groups, genders, and nationalities". They argue for human-subjects-research-style ethical review and fiduciary duties (abstract). — [Celedonia et al. 2021, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8284882/)
- Barnett & Torous 2019 (Annals, Ideas and Opinions; no abstract on PubMed, body **not seen**). Per news coverage: they argued Facebook's screening should meet clinical-research standards such as external review and informed consent, and that users "may be experimented on". — [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30743261/); secondary: [STAT News, 11 Feb 2019](https://www.statnews.com/2019/02/11/facebook-suicide-prevention-tools-ethics-privacy/), [Global News](https://globalnews.ca/news/4949331/facebook-suicide-prevention-tool-annals-internal-medicine/amp/)
- Gomes de Andrade et al. 2018 (Facebook authors): abstract describes the program's evolution through "understanding, reactive reporting, queue prioritization, and proactive reporting", the use of AI/ML "to detect posts or live videos where someone might be expressing thoughts of suicide", and a section on "the competing values and interests that were at stake ... and how we reached ethical balances between them." Body **not seen** (Springer blocked automated access; open access CC BY). — [Semantic Scholar record with abstract](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1007/s13347-018-0336-0?fields=abstract); [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-018-0336-0)
- Base rates: Belsher et al. 2019 (17 cohort studies, 64 models, >14 million participants): "Global classification accuracy was good (≥0.80 in most models), while the predictive validity associated with a positive result for suicide mortality was extremely low (≤0.01 in most models)"; "their accuracy of predicting a future event is near 0." — [Belsher et al. 2019, PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30865249/)
- Franklin et al. 2017 meta-analysis (365 studies, 50 years): "prediction was only slightly better than chance for all outcomes ... predictive ability has not improved across 50 years of research". — [Franklin et al. 2017, PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27841450/)

### Inferences
- False positives at low base rates: the clinical models above work on rich medical records and still have PPV ≤1% for death by suicide. A phrase list plus an LLM question on poems, where dark imagery is the normal subject, will flag many people who are writing about grief, not planning harm. Illustrative arithmetic (hypothetical numbers): if 1 in 200 writers in a session were in acute risk, a screen with 90% sensitivity and 90% specificity flags about 10.4% of sessions, and only about 4% of flags are true (0.45 / 10.4). For a pause that only offers phone numbers, a false positive costs a moment of friction. For a "detector" that reports or labels people, it costs what Marks and Celedonia list.
- Chilling effect on self-disclosure: poetry is a place where people write what they cannot say. If the tool claims to detect risk, writers who know or suspect it may self-censor, the opposite of the purpose. The critiques' points on awareness and consent (Celedonia, Barnett & Torous) go further: a pause the writer can dismiss ("Escape continues"), with nothing stored, gives the person control. The scholarly sources above address platforms, not writing tools; this transfer is my inference.
- Consent and transparency: say plainly in the UI and the report what triggers the pause (phrases + a model question), that nothing is stored or sent, and that it can be wrong. That answers Marks' transparency and opt-out recommendations directly.
- Violence risk ("harm to others"): the cited literature is about suicide. Claims to detect intent to harm others add risks around policing and false accusation; no source here measured that, and it should not be claimed.
- Wording guidance for the report: "offers helplines when a text sounds like pain or danger" (safety net, no assessment) rather than "detects suicide risk", "screens", "identifies at-risk users", "predicts" or "intent detection".

### Gaps
- Barnett & Torous 2019 and Gomes de Andrade et al. 2018 full texts not opened; claims about them beyond metadata and the quoted abstract come from secondary sources.
- No empirical study found on chilling effects of risk screening in creative-writing or journaling tools; none was searched exhaustively.
- Facebook's later EU rollout (it reportedly extended a limited version to the EU/UK around 2020) was not verified; do not state it.
