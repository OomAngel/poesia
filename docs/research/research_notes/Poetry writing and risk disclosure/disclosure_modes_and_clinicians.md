# Mode of asking, disclosure of suicidal ideation and self-harm, and how well clinicians detect and predict suicide risk

Claim under test: "people disclose distress and suicidal thoughts more honestly when writing or when no person is listening than in a conversation with a clinician." Written for the technical report of PoesIA, a poetry-writing app with a safety pause.

Notes compiled 2026-10-08. Every DOI below was resolved on Crossref (api.crossref.org) on 2026-10-08, and the bibliographic details given are Crossref's. "Seen" levels: **[full text]** = I read the article text (or the accepted or preprint manuscript, where noted); **[abstract]** = I read only the PubMed or publisher abstract, so numbers come from the abstract; **[not seen]** = I could open neither the full text nor an abstract, so nothing from it is reported as verified.

Short answer: the claim holds only in a narrower form. **When no one can see or act on the answers (anonymity, confidentiality, no consequences), people report more sensitive information, including suicidal ideation (SI).** "Writing" as such, or "no person listening", is not shown to be the active ingredient. People also deny SI on written and online questionnaires. In one study, mental health professionals received the most accurate disclosures. Animated virtual interviewers do not reliably beat humans. No study tests creative or poetry writing as a way of disclosing suicidality.

## Q1. Self-administered vs interviewer-administered modes: how much more sensitive information is reported, and is there evidence specific to suicidal ideation and self-harm?

### Takeaway
Self-administration raises reports of stigmatised behaviours by a modest, consistent amount. The median increase was about 30% for illicit drug use, and up to about 3x for the most stigmatised items with audio-CASI (audio computer-assisted self-interviewing). For SI specifically, the best evidence is observational: comparisons of self-report with clinician-documented SI in the same patients, and anonymous vs identified screening. Agreement between the two is low, and the self-report or anonymous route usually yields more SI, in one study 2–4 times more. I found no meta-analysis that randomises interview vs self-report for SI. Mode effects also run the other way in some samples. Anonymity, not writing or computerisation, appears to drive the effect.

### Cited Findings

**General survey-methods evidence (not suicide-specific)**
- **Tourangeau R, Yan T (2007). Sensitive questions in surveys. *Psychological Bulletin* 133(5):859–883. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.133.5.859 [full text].** This is a narrative review with two small meta-analyses; it does not study suicide or clinical populations. Its Table 2 pools 7 randomised field experiments on illicit drug use, comparing self-administered with interviewer-administered modes (SAQ, IVR, ACASI vs face-to-face or telephone): "Almost without exception … a higher proportion of respondents reported illicit drug use when the questions were self-administered … The median increase from self-administration is 30%." Individual prevalence ratios range from 0.91 to 2.84; for example, ACASI vs face-to-face for past-year cocaine use gave a ratio of 2.84. Its own meta-analysis of *computerised vs paper* self-administration (10 studies) found a mean log odds ratio of 0.08 (SE 0.07), which is **nonsignificant**. For personality and social-desirability scales (4 studies) it found −0.02. The authors conclude that misreporting is "largely situational" and "motivated": respondents edit answers "to avoid embarrassing themselves in the presence of an interviewer or to avoid repercussions from third parties." Limits: mostly US general-population surveys of drug use, sex and abortion, and higher reporting is *assumed* to mean more accurate reporting, without validation against a criterion. — [Tourangeau & Yan 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.5.859); full text read at [archived PDF](http://web.archive.org/web/2020/http://www.learnlab.org/research/wiki/images/a/a8/Tourangeau_SensitiveQuestions.pdf)
- Tourangeau & Yan's account of **Richman WL, Kiesler S, Weisband S, Drasgow F (1999). A meta-analytic study of social desirability distortion in computer-administered questionnaires, traditional questionnaires, and interviews. *Journal of Applied Psychology* 84(5):754–775. doi:10.1037/0021-9010.84.5.754 [Richman itself not seen; figures as reported by Tourangeau & Yan]:** computer instruments reduced social-desirability distortion relative to *face-to-face interviews* (mean effect −.19, which in their sign convention means more reporting of psychiatric symptoms and undesirable behaviours to the computer). Against *paper* questionnaires the effect was about 0 (0.05, n.s.). **When data were not anonymous, computer administration *decreased* reporting relative to paper**, which Tourangeau & Yan read as a "big brother" effect. — [Tourangeau & Yan 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.5.859); [Richman et al. 1999 DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.84.5.754)
- **Gnambs T, Kaspar K (2015). Disclosure of sensitive behaviors across self-administered survey modes: a meta-analysis. *Behavior Research Methods* 47(4):1237–1259. doi:10.3758/s13428-014-0533-4 [abstract; full text not reachable].** It pooled 460 effect sizes (total N = 125,672). It compares **computerised vs paper self-administration only; it does not compare self-administration with an interviewer.** Computerised surveys "led to significantly more reporting of socially undesirable behaviors," most strongly for "highly sensitive behaviors" and for surveys "administered individually." "Moderator analyses did not identify interviewer effects or benefits of audio-enhanced computer surveys." I could not verify the pooled odds ratio or whether any suicide or self-harm items were included. **Correction to how it is often cited:** it is not evidence that people disclose more to a computer *than to a clinician*. — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25410404/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-014-0533-4)
- **Turner CF, Ku L, Rogers SM, Lindberg LD, Pleck JH, Sonenstein FL (1998). Adolescent sexual behavior, drug use, and violence: increased reporting with computer survey technology. *Science* 280(5365):867–873. doi:10.1126/science.280.5365.867 [abstract].** This RCT randomised 1,690 respondents in the 1995 National Survey of Adolescent Males to audio-CASI or a paper self-administered questionnaire. With audio-CASI, reports of male-male sex, injection drug use and sex with IV drug users were "higher by factors of 3 or more." It did not cover suicide. — [Turner et al. 1998](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.280.5365.867)

**Suicide- and SI-specific comparisons**
- **Warner CH, Appenzeller GN, Grieger T, Belenkiy S, Breitbach J, Parker J, Warner CM, Hoge C (2011). Importance of anonymity to encourage honest reporting in mental health screening after combat deployment. *Archives of General Psychiatry* 68(10):1065–1071. doi:10.1001/archgenpsychiatry.2011.112 [abstract].** Population: 1,712 of 2,500 invited US Army infantry soldiers (68.5% response rate) from one brigade in 2008. They completed the routine identified Post-Deployment Health Assessment (PDHA), which is written and goes to command and medical records, and simultaneously an anonymous survey with the same items, including the PHQ-9 SI item. "Reporting of depression, PTSD, **suicidal ideation**, and interest in receiving care were **2-fold to 4-fold higher on the anonymous survey**." 20.3% of those who screened positive said they were uncomfortable answering honestly on the routine screen. **Both arms were written self-report**, so the contrast is identified vs anonymous, not writing vs talking. Limits: one brigade, military career consequences, and no criterion standard. — [Warner et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1001/archgenpsychiatry.2011.112)
- **McClure JR, Tal I, Macera CA, Ji M, Nievergelt CM, Lee SY, Kayman J, Zisook S (2018). Agreement between self and psychiatrist reporting of suicidal ideation at a Veterans Administration psychiatric emergency clinic. *Depression and Anxiety* 35(11):1114–1121. doi:10.1002/da.22813 [abstract].** N = 377 veterans at a VA psychiatric emergency clinic. 53% self-reported SI on a survey, but SI appeared in only 21% of psychiatrist notes. The two sources disagreed in 44% of cases, and in 97% of those disagreements the self-report was the more severe. Of the 120 veterans with self-reported SI whom the psychiatrist documented as having none, 26% reported a suicide plan and 15% plan preparations. Limit: the comparison is clinician *documentation*, not what the patient said aloud. — [McClure et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/da.22813)
- **Vera-Varela C, Manrique Mirón PC, Barrigón ML, Álvarez-García R, Portillo P, Chamorro J; MEmind Study Group; Baca-García E (2022). Low level of agreement between self-report and clinical assessment of passive suicidal ideation. *Archives of Suicide Research* 26(4):1895–1910. doi:10.1080/13811118.2021.1945984 [abstract].** 648 Spanish outpatients. Clinicians recorded wish for death, and patients completed a self-report within 24 h. Kappa was 0.072. In 56.4% (n = 366) the clinician recorded no death-related ideas while the patient's self-report said they had no desire to live. The construct is *passive* ideation only. — [Vera-Varela et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/13811118.2021.1945984)
- **Nobile B, Belzeaux R, Aouizerate B, … Courtet P, Olié E (2023). Clinical characteristics associated with discrepancies between self- and clinician-rated suicidal ideation in patients with bipolar disorder (FACE-BD cohort). *Psychiatry Research* 321:115055. doi:10.1016/j.psychres.2023.115055 [abstract].** Patients with bipolar disorder who had SI on either the QIDS-SR item 12 (self-rated) or MADRS item 10 (clinician-rated): 25.6% (n = 130) were concordant and **74.4% (n = 377) discordant**. Discordance was most associated with depression severity, "especially in patients with SI identified only with the self-rated questionnaire"; clinicians may miss SI in patients with low depression. — [Nobile et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2023.115055)
- **Kaplan ML, Asnis GM, Sanderson WC, Keswani L, De Lecuona JM, Joseph S (1994). Suicide assessment: clinical interview vs. self-report. *Journal of Clinical Psychology* 50(2):294–298. doi:10.1002/1097-4679(199403)50:2<294::AID-JCLP2270500224>3.0.CO;2-R [abstract].** Psychiatric outpatients gave "a generally high level of agreement" between self-report and face-to-face interview. The single exception was *recent* SI, where "patients tended to disclose more on the self-report form." The abstract gives no N. — [Kaplan et al. 1994](https://doi.org/10.1002/1097-4679(199403)50:2%3C294::AID-JCLP2270500224%3E3.0.CO;2-R)
- **Contrary direction: Joiner TE Jr, Rudd MD, Rajab MH (1999). Agreement between self- and clinician-rated suicidal symptoms in a clinical sample of young adults: explaining discrepancies. *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* 67(2):171–176. doi:10.1037/0022-006X.67.2.171 [abstract].** N = 328 young adults referred for SI or suicidal behaviour. Discrepancy was high, but "clinicians were likely to see patients as high in suicidal tendency, whereas patients were less likely to see themselves as such." Patients' self-ratings "contained considerable predictive value" for later symptoms. **In this sample, self-report showed *less* suicidality than the clinician.** — [Joiner et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006x.67.2.171)
- **Velting DM, Rathus JH, Asnis GM (1998). Asking adolescents to explain discrepancies in self-reported suicidality. *Suicide and Life-Threatening Behavior* 28(2):187–196 [abstract; no DOI in PubMed; PMID 9674078].** 48 adolescents gave attempt histories by self-report and then, about 5 days later, by semistructured interview. 50% gave discrepant reports. The abstract does not say which direction dominated. — [PubMed 9674078](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9674078/)
- **Hallford DJ, Rusanov D, Winestone B, Kaplan R, Fuller-Tyszkiewicz M, Melvin G (2023). Disclosure of suicidal ideation and behaviours: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prevalence. *Clinical Psychology Review* 101:102272. doi:10.1016/j.cpr.2023.102272 [abstract and PsyArXiv preprint full text].** k = 94 studies, N = 1,044,629. Pooled prevalence of having disclosed SI or suicidal behaviour to anyone was **45.9%** (95% CI 41.9–49.9; prediction interval 12.3–81.8; I² = 99.9%). The study's communication approach moderated prevalence (Q[2] = 12.7, p = .002): **online written 70%** (95% CI 57.6–73.9; k = 11) vs **verbal 49%** (44–53.9; k = 38) vs **written in person 47.1%** (36.5–57.8; k = 8). Two cautions: (a) the outcome is *past disclosure to other people*, and "communication approach" is how the *study* collected that report; it is not the mode of the original disclosure. (b) The authors say the online advantage "may be due to anonymity or less fear of consequences … or may indicate a stronger self-selection bias." **Writing in person did not beat verbal report**, which counts against "writing" as the active ingredient. Disclosure was lower among suicide decedents (40.8%). — [Hallford et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2023.102272); [preprint](https://psyarxiv.com/b4rjf/download)

**Nondisclosure also happens on written and online self-report**
- **Louzon SA, Bossarte R, McCarthy JF, Katz IR (2016). Does suicidal ideation as measured by the PHQ-9 predict suicide among VA patients? *Psychiatric Services* 67(5):517–522. doi:10.1176/appi.ps.201500149 [abstract].** 447,245 PHQ-9 assessments, a *self-report questionnaire*, at the Veterans Health Administration (VHA), 2009–10, linked to the National Death Index. Item 9 hazard ratios were: "several days" 1.75 (1.24–2.46), "more than half the days" 2.15 (1.32–3.51), "nearly every day" 2.85 (1.81–4.47). Yet **71.6% of suicides occurred among patients who answered "not at all"** on their most recent PHQ-9. — [Louzon et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ps.201500149)
- **Podlogar MC, Rogers ML, Chiurliza B, Hom MA, Tzoneva M, Joiner T (2016). Who are we missing? Nondisclosure in online suicide risk screening questionnaires. *Psychological Assessment* 28(8):963–974. doi:10.1037/pas0000242 [abstract].** Three undergraduate samples (n = 1,306; 694; 172). The few who skipped online suicide items (n = 7, 6, 7) "scored significantly higher than Deniers and similarly to Endorsers" on related suicide-risk measures. Online self-report therefore still contains concealers. — [Podlogar et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/pas0000242)
- **Podlogar MC, Joiner TE (2020). Allowing for nondisclosure in high suicide risk groups. *Assessment* 27(3):547–559. doi:10.1177/1073191119845495 [abstract].** Samples: military and veterans (n = 135), men over 50 (n = 187), LGBTQ young adults (n = 140). An explicit "prefer not to disclose" option "minimally affected screening sensitivity and specificity," but higher-risk participants chose it more often, and choosing it was associated with "past unhelpful reactions" and "avoidance of stigmatized consequences." — [Podlogar & Joiner 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/1073191119845495)
- **Klimes-Dougan B, Mirza SA, Babkin E, Lanning C (2022). Biased reporting of past self-injurious thoughts and behaviors: a literature review. *Journal of Affective Disorders* 308:596–606. doi:10.1016/j.jad.2022.04.073 [abstract].** Across 19 longitudinal samples, a lifetime report of self-injurious thoughts or behaviours (SITB) was *retracted* at a later assessment with a median of 33% and a weighted mean of 39% (95% CI 37–41%). So even repeated self-report is unstable. — [Klimes-Dougan et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2022.04.073)
- **Millner AJ, Lee MD, Nock MK (2015). Single-item measurement of suicidal behaviors: validity and consequences of misclassification. *PLOS ONE* 10(10):e0141606. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0141606 [abstract].** Online survey, N > 1,500. Of those endorsing a single-item SI question, 8.8% described thoughts that did not meet standard definitions of SI; for attempts the figure was 11.3%. Written self-report also produces *false positives*. — [Millner et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0141606)

### Inferences
- The robust pattern is that **anonymity and freedom from consequences raise reporting**. Evidence: Warner (anonymous vs identified, both written); Richman's finding, as reported by Tourangeau & Yan, that computers *lower* reporting when answers are not anonymous; Hallford's online-written > in-person-written ≈ verbal. The medium (writing, computer) has a small or zero effect once anonymity is held constant (Tourangeau & Yan's computer-vs-paper meta-analysis was nonsignificant).
- For a PoesIA safety pause, this cuts both ways. A poem written privately is closer to the "anonymous" condition. Once users learn that certain words trigger a pause or a redirect, the app becomes a "consequence", the same thing that makes people conceal SI from clinicians (see Q3). The technical report should not claim users will disclose more to the app unless the app also avoids consequences that feel punitive.
- Discordance between self-report and clinician ratings is the norm (kappa ≈ 0.07; 44–74% discordant). The usual direction is self-report > clinician note, but not always (Joiner 1999). "More reported" does not equal "more honest": Millner shows over-endorsement, Klimes-Dougan shows retractions.

### Gaps
- No randomised trial or meta-analysis that assigns people to interview vs self-administered (paper or computer) assessment of **SI or self-harm** and compares prevalence against a criterion. Searched PubMed with title/abstract strings combining suicid*, self-report, questionnaire, interview, clinician, computer, tablet, audio computer-assisted self-interview, emergency department and primary care; most hits were concordance studies, listed above.
- No emergency-department or primary-care RCT comparing tablet self-screening with nurse- or clinician-asked screening for SI was found in my searches. Absence is not proven; only PubMed was searched.
- Gnambs & Kaspar: the pooled odds ratio, the item list (whether any depression or suicide items) and the moderator coefficients could not be verified; the Springer full text redirected to login.
- Richman et al. 1999: read only as summarised by Tourangeau & Yan.
- **No study found that tests creative or poetic writing as a mode of disclosing SI.** Expressive-writing research (Pennebaker paradigm) is outside this note.

## Q2. Disclosure to computers, virtual agents and chatbots

### Takeaway
One lab study (Lucas et al. 2014) found that *believing* a virtual human was automated, rather than human-operated, lowered fear of disclosure and raised observer-rated willingness to disclose. A military field follow-up (Lucas et al. 2017) found more PTSD-symptom reporting to a virtual human than on the official form, but only marginally more than on an anonymous form. A survey experiment (Lind et al. 2013) found that **animated virtual interviewers did not increase disclosure**; faceless voice-only self-administration did best. The often-cited chatbot study (Ho, Hancock & Miner 2018) found **equivalence** between chatbot and human partners, not more disclosure to chatbots. None of these measured SI disclosure against a criterion.

### Cited Findings
- **Lucas GM, Gratch J, King A, Morency L-P (2014). It's only a computer: Virtual humans increase willingness to disclose. *Computers in Human Behavior* 37:94–100. doi:10.1016/j.chb.2014.04.043 [abstract and highlights only; full text not seen].** Participants were interviewed by the same rapport-building virtual human (VH) in a health-screening interview and told either that it was operated by humans or that it was automated. In the "computer" framing they "reported lower fear of self-disclosure, lower impression management, displayed their sadness more intensely, and were rated by observers as more willing to disclose." **What was measured:** belief about who controls the *same* agent; outcomes were self-reported fear and impression management, observer ratings and facial expression. **What was not measured:** a comparison with a human clinician, or the accuracy or validity of disclosed content. The N and effect sizes are not verified here (full text paywalled). Citation as given by the user is correct. — [Lucas et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2014.04.043)
- **Lucas GM, Rizzo A, Gratch J, Scherer S, Stratou G, Boberg J, Morency L-P (2017). Reporting mental health symptoms: breaking down barriers to care with virtual human interviewers. *Frontiers in Robotics and AI* 4:51. doi:10.3389/frobt.2017.00051 [full text via WebFetch].** Study 1: 24 analysed of 29 Colorado National Guard members back from Afghanistan. PTSD symptoms reported were: VH M = 0.79 vs official PDHA 0.25 vs anonymised PDHA 0.33 (F(2,23) = 4.29, p = .02). Study 2: 126 service members and veterans. VH 1.21 vs anonymised PDHA 1.05, t(125) = 1.76, **p = .08 (n.s.)**; this became significant only in the below-median PCL subgroup or with a PCL covariate. The authors' limits: VH questions were open-ended (a format difference), order was fixed (anonymised PDHA always first), and the sample was military only. — [Lucas et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2017.00051)
- **Lind LH, Schober MF, Conrad FG, Reichert H (2013). Why do survey respondents disclose more when computers ask the questions? *Public Opinion Quarterly* 77(4):888–935. doi:10.1093/poq/nft038 [abstract via publisher page].** Lab experiment, N = 235, four modes: voice-only ACASI, face-to-face human, and two animated virtual interviewers (high and low facial motion), all with the same human voice. "Disclosure for most questions was greater under ACASI than in any of the other modes, even though the two virtual interview modes involved computerized self-administration." **This is a failure of the "virtual agent increases disclosure" idea: adding a face removed the computer advantage.** — [Lind et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nft038)
- **Ho A, Hancock J, Miner AS (2018). Psychological, relational, and emotional effects of self-disclosure after conversations with a chatbot. *Journal of Communication* 68(4):712–733. doi:10.1093/joc/jqy026 [full text, PMC6074615].** Design: 2 (emotional vs factual disclosure) × 2 (perceived partner: chatbot vs person). The "chatbot" was a **Wizard-of-Oz: trained human confederates in every condition.** 128 university participants (68.75% women, mean age 22), N = 98 after exclusions; 13 were excluded for suspicion about the partner's identity. Result: "Participants who disclosed to chatbots experienced as many emotional, relational, and psychological benefits as participants who disclosed to a human partner." Emotional disclosure beat factual on feeling better (F[1,94] = 13.36, partial η² = .12), but there was **no main effect of partner and no interaction on any outcome, including disclosure intimacy and perceived understanding** (the "Equivalence Hypothesis"). The only partner difference was that participants used simpler language with the "chatbot". **Correction to how it is popularly cited:** it does not show that people disclose *more* to chatbots. It was not about SI; the participants were students. — [Ho et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/joc/jqy026); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6074615/)
- **Pickard MD, Roster CA, Chen Y (2016). Revealing sensitive information in personal interviews: Is self-disclosure easier with humans or avatars and under what conditions? *Computers in Human Behavior* 65:23–30. doi:10.1016/j.chb.2016.08.004 [not seen; publisher returned 403 and the abstract is withheld from Semantic Scholar].** DOI and bibliographic data verified. Findings are not reported here as verified. A Semantic Scholar machine summary says topic sensitivity affected whether people preferred a human or an avatar interviewer, which suggests a conditional rather than a blanket effect. — [Pickard et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2016.08.004)
- **Greist JH, Gustafson DH, Stauss FF, Rowse GL, Laughren TP, Chiles JA (1973). A computer interview for suicide-risk prediction. *American Journal of Psychiatry* 130(12):1327–1332. doi:10.1176/ajp.130.12.1327 [not seen; PubMed has no abstract].** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1176/ajp.130.12.1327)
- **Erdman HP, Greist JH, Gustafson DH, Taves JE, Klein MH (1987). Suicide risk prediction by computer interview: a prospective study. *Journal of Clinical Psychiatry* 48(12):464–467 [abstract; no DOI in PubMed; PMID 3693329].** N = 52 patients. The computer was better at predicting attempters (p = .001), clinicians better at predicting non-attempters (p = .01), and the difference in overall ROC discrimination was nonsignificant. — [PubMed 3693329](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3693329/)
- **LLM-era survey and help-seeking evidence:** **McBain RK, Cantor JH, Breslau J, et al. (2026). AI chatbot use and disclosure for mental health among US adolescents and young adults. *JAMA Pediatrics* 180(8):884–890. doi:10.1001/jamapediatrics.2026.2015 [abstract].** Nationally representative survey, November 2025, ages 12–21, unweighted n = 1,009. 19.2% had used AI chatbots for mental-health advice, 42.8% of those at least monthly, and 91.7% rated the advice somewhat or very helpful. 63.3% had not told anyone they used chatbots this way. Users were *more* likely to have spoken with a physician about mental health in the prior 6 months (aOR 1.89). It measures use, not the accuracy of SI disclosure. — [McBain et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2026.2015)
- **Heo R, McBride LE, Parrish EM, Fulginiti A, Taylor CT, Depp C (2026). Seeking help from chatbots for suicide thoughts: associations with other help seeking sources and mental health symptoms. *medRxiv* preprint, doi:10.64898/2026.08.12.26360318 [abstract; NOT peer reviewed].** N = 58 adults with clinically significant depression or anxiety. They reported **lower** intentions to seek help for suicidal thoughts from chatbots than from informal or formal sources. Chatbot intentions correlated only with intentions to use anonymous online sources. — [Heo et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.08.12.26360318)

### Inferences
- The virtual-agent literature supports a narrow point: **feeling unobserved and unjudged** lowers impression management (Lucas 2014; Lind 2013 shows that a *face* can cancel the effect). It does not show that people tell an agent the truth about suicidality more than a clinician; no study here validated disclosed SI against an outcome.
- For PoesIA, the closest analogue is a faceless text interface (as in Lind's ACASI and Hallford's online-written condition). An anthropomorphised companion persona is not supported as a disclosure booster.
- Equivalence (Ho 2018) means that the benefits of emotional disclosure do not depend on a human listener. That bears on the *wellbeing* value of writing, not on detecting risk.

### Gaps
- No replication of Lucas 2014 with suicide-specific items and a criterion measure was found. Kang & Gratch's ICT papers appeared in search but were not reviewed.
- Pickard 2016 and the Lucas 2014 full texts were not accessible (Elsevier 403 or paywall).
- No RCT found comparing disclosure of SI to an LLM chatbot with disclosure to a clinician.

## Q3. Denial of suicidal ideation to clinicians before suicide or attempt, contact with services, and reasons for non-disclosure

### Takeaway
Denial is common. About half of decedents denied SI in the week or month before death (Obegi 2021, narrative review of 22 papers). In two chart-review samples, 78% (Busch 2003, inpatients) and about two-thirds (Berman 2018) denied SI at their last clinical contact. Most people who die by suicide saw a health service in the prior year (about 80% primary care; 44% in the last month). Fear of consequences is the main reason for concealment: hospitalisation, loss of autonomy, stigma. **The same denial shows up on written screening questionnaires (PHQ-9 item 9), so concealment is not confined to spoken conversation with a clinician.** In one survey people reported their *most accurate* disclosure to mental health professionals. Question phrasing by clinicians biases answers toward "no."

### Cited Findings
- **Busch KA, Fawcett J, Jacobs DG (2003). Clinical correlates of inpatient suicide. *Journal of Clinical Psychiatry* 64(1):14–19. doi:10.4088/JCP.v64n0105 [abstract].** Charts of 76 patients who died by suicide in hospital or immediately after discharge (US). 39% (30/76) were admitted for SI, but "**78% denied suicidal ideation at their last communication about this**." 28% (21) had a no-suicide contract. Of the 45 on precautions, 51% (23) were on 15-minute checks or 1:1 observation. 79% (60) met criteria for severe or extreme anxiety and/or agitation. Limits: retrospective chart review of what was *documented*; **no comparison group** (the authors say one is needed); a single selected sample. The denial figure is a share of decedents, not a disclosure rate in a defined denominator. User citation details are correct. — [Busch et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.4088/JCP.v64n0105)
- **Berman AL (2018). Risk factors proximate to suicide and suicide risk assessment in the context of denied suicide ideation. *Suicide and Life-Threatening Behavior* 48(3):340–352. doi:10.1111/sltb.12351 (epub 2017) [abstract].** Records of the last 30 days of 157 patients who died by suicide. "**Two-thirds of patients denied having SI when last asked and one-half of these patients were dead by suicide within 2 days.**" Deniers looked "quite similar in charted diagnoses, symptoms, behaviors, and environmental circumstances" to affirmers. Limits: a selected sample whose sourcing the abstract does not describe, and no living comparison group. — [Berman 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/sltb.12351)
- **Obegi JH (2021). How common is recent denial of suicidal ideation among ideators, attempters, and suicide decedents? A literature review. *General Hospital Psychiatry* 72:92–95. doi:10.1016/j.genhosppsych.2021.07.009 [abstract].** Narrative review of 22 papers from 2000–2021: "About 50% of ideators denied suicidal ideation (SI) during interviews … about half of decedents denied SI in the previous week or month before suicide, whereas about 30% denied SI in the previous week or month before a suicide attempt." This is not a formal meta-analysis. — [Obegi 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.genhosppsych.2021.07.009)
- **Richards JE, Whiteside U, Ludman EJ, Pabiniak C, Kirlin B, Hidalgo R, Simon G (2019). Understanding why patients may not report suicidal ideation at a health care visit prior to a suicide attempt: a qualitative study. *Psychiatric Services* 70(1):40–45. doi:10.1176/appi.ps.201800342 [abstract].** Kaiser Permanente Washington patients who answered **"not at all" on PHQ-9 item 9, a self-report questionnaire**, and then attempted suicide within 60 days. 26 of 42 sampled agreed to semistructured interviews. Themes: they were either not experiencing SI at the time of screening, or they "feared the outcome of disclosure, including stigma, overreaction, and loss of autonomy." Heavy episodic drinking featured in unplanned attempts. **Correction to how it may be cited:** this is about non-disclosure on a *written questionnaire* in routine care, not about face-to-face clinician questioning versus self-report. Qualitative, small n, and 38% of those sampled declined. — [Richards et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ps.201800342)
- **Simon GE, Rutter CM, Peterson D, Oliver M, Whiteside U, Operskalski B, Ludman EJ (2013). Does response on the PHQ-9 depression questionnaire predict subsequent suicide attempt or suicide death? *Psychiatric Services* 64(12):1195–1202. doi:10.1176/appi.ps.201200587 [abstract].** 84,418 outpatients aged 13 or older completed 207,265 PHQ-9s (2007–2011); outcomes were 709 attempts and 46 deaths. One-year attempt risk was 0.4% for "not at all" vs 4% for "nearly every day"; suicide death risk was 0.03% vs 0.3%. Item 9 remained a predictor after adjustment. **Even the highest self-reported SI group had a 99.7% one-year probability of not dying by suicide.** — [Simon et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ps.201200587)
- **Hom MA, Stanley IH, Podlogar MC, Joiner TE (2017). "Are you having thoughts of suicide?" Examining experiences with disclosing and denying suicidal ideation. *Journal of Clinical Psychology* 73(10):1382–1392. doi:10.1002/jclp.22440 [abstract].** N = 306 US undergraduates with lifetime SI, web survey. 63.1% had been asked about suicidal thoughts before, most often by health care providers. "**Individuals reported the greatest accuracy of ideation disclosure to mental health professionals.**" Stigma-related concerns were the main barrier; wanting emotional support and wanting to be understood were facilitators. **This counts against the claim:** a trained clinician was the context of the *most* accurate self-reported disclosure. Limits: student sample, retrospective self-rated "accuracy". — [Hom et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/jclp.22440)
- **Blanchard M, Farber BA (2020). "It is never okay to talk about suicide": Patients' reasons for concealing suicidal ideation in psychotherapy. *Psychotherapy Research* 30(1):124–136. doi:10.1080/10503307.2018.1543977 [abstract].** 66 psychotherapy clients who had concealed SI wrote short essays. **70%** cited fear of unwanted practical impacts, chiefly **involuntary hospitalisation**; shame or embarrassment was secondary. "Nearly half … said they would be more honest only if the threat of hospitalization was somehow reduced or controlled." — [Blanchard & Farber 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/10503307.2018.1543977)
- **McCabe R, Sterno I, Priebe S, Barnes R, Byng R (2017). How do healthcare professionals interview patients to assess suicide risk? *BMC Psychiatry* 17:122. doi:10.1186/s12888-017-1212-7 [abstract; open access].** Conversation analysis of 319 video-recorded UK secondary-care outpatient visits, which contained 83 SI exchanges in 77 visits, plus 6 primary-care cases. 75% of SI questions were negatively phrased ("No thoughts of harming yourself?"). Patients were more likely to say they were not suicidal after negative phrasing (χ² = 7.2, p = .016), but positive phrasing did not raise "yes" answers. 25% of patients answered with a narrative conveying ambivalence; psychiatrists pushed for a yes/no and moved on after "no." **Part of clinician non-detection comes from how the question is asked, not from the spoken mode as such.** — [McCabe et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-017-1212-7)
- **Stene-Larsen K, Reneflot A (2019). Contact with primary and mental health care prior to suicide: a systematic review of the literature from 2000 to 2017. *Scandinavian Journal of Public Health* 47(1):9–17. doi:10.1177/1403494817746274 (epub 2017) [abstract].** 44 studies (36 primary care, 14 mental health). Average contact with primary care was **80% in the year before suicide and 44% in the final month**. Mental health care contact was 57% lifetime and 31% in the final 12 months. Contact was higher among women and people over 50. User citation is correct; note that it is a systematic review with averaged rates, not a pooled meta-analysis. — [Stene-Larsen & Reneflot 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/1403494817746274)
- **Luoma JB, Martin CE, Pearson JL (2002). Contact with mental health and primary care providers before suicide: a review of the evidence. *American Journal of Psychiatry* 159(6):909–916. doi:10.1176/appi.ajp.159.6.909 [abstract].** 40 studies. About 3 in 4 had primary-care contact within the year and on average 45% within one month; about one-third had mental health contact within the year and 1 in 5 within a month. — [Luoma et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.159.6.909)
- **Ahmedani BK, Simon GE, Stewart C, et al. (2014). Health care contacts in the year before suicide death. *Journal of General Internal Medicine* 29(6):870–877. doi:10.1007/s11606-014-2767-3 [abstract].** 5,894 decedents in 8 US health systems, 2000–2010. 83% had health care in the prior year, but half had no mental-health diagnosis, and only 24% had one in the final 4 weeks. — [Ahmedani et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11606-014-2767-3)
- **Pompili M, Belvederi Murri M, Patti S, Innamorati M, Lester D, Girardi P, Amore M (2016). The communication of suicidal intentions: a meta-analysis. *Psychological Medicine* 46(11):2239–2253. doi:10.1017/S0033291716000696 [abstract].** 36 studies, 14,601 suicides. **44.5%** (95% CI 35.4–53.8; I² = 98.8%) had communicated suicidal intent. Prevalence was lower when only verbal communication was detected, which suggests written and other channels add cases. In 7 case-control studies the OR for suicide was 4.66 (3.00–7.25), with "insufficient accuracy." — [Pompili et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291716000696)

### Inferences
- The best-supported reading of "people hide SI from clinicians" is **concealment driven by fear of consequences** (Blanchard & Farber 70%; Richards; Podlogar & Joiner), **plus closed, negatively framed questions** (McCabe). Nothing here shows that the spoken modality itself suppresses disclosure.
- Concealment carries over to self-report whenever consequences are attached. Of VA suicides, 71.6% had answered "not at all" on a written PHQ-9 item 9; Richards' sample denied on paper. A PoesIA safety pause that people perceive as consequential could produce the same concealment, for example by changing what users write once they learn what triggers it.
- Point for the technical report: an app that never gets a "no" (because it does not ask) and that reads free text instead has a different failure mode. It can miss people who do not write about their state and over-flag dark imagery. No study here measures how either error behaves for poetry.

### Gaps
- Busch 2003 and Berman 2018: full texts not seen. How "denial" was operationalised (documented statement vs clinician paraphrase) cannot be described here.
- No meta-analysis specifically of *denial at the last contact* was found beyond Obegi's narrative review.
- Hom et al. 2017: the abstract gives no per-prober accuracy percentages; full text not seen.

## Q4. Prediction: how well do risk factors, clinicians and tools predict suicide, and what does this imply for any screening tool, including an app?

### Takeaway
Single risk factors predict suicidal thoughts and behaviours only slightly better than chance (weighted AUC ≈ 0.57–0.58; Franklin 2017). Clinical "high-risk" categories carry pooled ORs of about 5–7, but with sensitivity around 53–56%. PPV for suicide is about 0.4–5.5% depending on setting. Large-scale ML models discriminate well overall (AUC ≥ 0.80) but have PPV ≤ 1% for death. Even expressed SI has a PPV of 0.3% for suicide outside psychiatric settings. **Prevalence caps PPV.** Any screener, an app included, will produce mostly false positives for death, and a negative screen gives little reassurance.

### Cited Findings
- **Franklin JC, Ribeiro JD, Fox KR, Bentley KH, Kleiman EM, Huang X, Musacchio KM, Jaroszewski AC, Chang BP, Nock MK (2017). Risk factors for suicidal thoughts and behaviors: a meta-analysis of 50 years of research. *Psychological Bulletin* 143(2):187–232. doi:10.1037/bul0000084 [full text].** 365 longitudinal studies, 3,428 risk-factor effect sizes. Overall weighted mean ORs: ideation **1.50** (1.47–1.54; I² = 90.5%), attempt **1.51** (1.49–1.54), death **1.50** (1.46–1.56; I² = 98.5%). Trim-and-fill brings ideation down to 1.26 and attempt to 1.30. Diagnostic accuracy: attempt wAUC **0.58** (sensitivity 0.26, specificity 0.75); death wAUC **0.57** (sensitivity 0.09, specificity 0.81). Prediction did not improve across 50 years, and longer follow-ups did not help. **What it measured:** single risk factors, mostly dichotomous and tested one at a time, over follow-ups averaging about 10 years. It did **not** measure clinicians' bedside judgement or short-term, near-term risk. It is popularly misquoted as "clinicians predict suicide at chance". The authors call for ML algorithms. User citation is correct. — [Franklin et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000084); full text read at [gwern.net copy](https://gwern.net/doc/psychiatry/2017-franklin.pdf)
- **Large M, Kaneson M, Myles N, Myles H, Gunaratne P, Ryan C (2016). Meta-analysis of longitudinal cohort studies of suicide risk assessment among psychiatric patients: heterogeneity in results and lack of improvement over time. *PLOS ONE* 11(6):e0156322. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0156322 [abstract; open access].** 53 samples from 37 studies. Pooled OR for suicide in high- vs lower-risk groups was **4.84** (3.79–6.20; I² = 93.3). Over a mean follow-up of 63 months, 5.5% of high-risk and 0.9% of lower-risk patients died by suicide. **Sensitivity 56%, specificity 79%.** Evidence of publication bias inflating the OR, and no improvement over time. Competing interest: the authors give expert evidence in court on suicide. — [Large et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0156322)
- **Large M, Myles N, Myles H, Corderoy A, Weiser M, Davidson M, Ryan CJ (2018). Suicide risk assessment among psychiatric inpatients: a systematic review and meta-analysis of high-risk categories. *Psychological Medicine* 48(7):1119–1127. doi:10.1017/S0033291717002537 (epub 2017) [abstract].** 18 studies. Pooled OR **7.1** (4.2–12.2), with a prediction interval of 0.80–63.1 (I² = 88.1%). Sensitivity 53.1%, specificity 84.2%, AUC 0.83. **PPV 0.43%** (0.014–1.3%) across 6 cohort studies. Conclusion: "suicide risk models are not a suitable basis for clinical decisions in inpatient settings." — [Large et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291717002537)
- **Carter G, Milner A, McGill K, Pirkis J, Kapur N, Spittal MJ (2017). Predicting suicidal behaviours using clinical instruments: systematic review and meta-analysis of positive predictive values for risk scales. *British Journal of Psychiatry* 210(6):387–395. doi:10.1192/bjp.bp.116.182717 [abstract].** Pooled PPVs for all scales: **suicide 5.5%** (3.9–7.9), self-harm 26.3% (21.8–31.3), self-harm plus suicide 35.9% (25.8–47.4). For self-harm in high-quality studies the PPV was 16.1%. "No 'high-risk' classification was clinically useful. Prevalence imposes a ceiling on PPV." Note: Kapur chaired the NICE self-harm guideline and Carter the RANZCP (Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Psychiatrists) one. — [Carter et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.bp.116.182717)
- **Chan MKY, Bhatti H, Meader N, Stockton S, Evans J, O'Connor RC, Kapur N, Kendall T (2016). Predicting suicide following self-harm: systematic review of risk factors and risk scales. *British Journal of Psychiatry* 209(4):277–283. doi:10.1192/bjp.bp.115.170050 [abstract].** After self-harm the risk factors were: prior self-harm HR 1.68, suicidal intent HR 2.7, physical illness HR 1.99, male sex HR 2.05. Only three scales had been studied (BHS, SIS, SSI), with PPVs of 1.3–16.7%. "No scales have sufficient evidence to support their use … may provide false reassurance and is, therefore, potentially dangerous." — [Chan et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.bp.115.170050)
- **Belsher BE, Smolenski DJ, Pruitt LD, Bush NE, Beech EH, Workman DE, Morgan RL, Evatt DP, Tucker J, Skopp NA (2019). Prediction models for suicide attempts and deaths: a systematic review and simulation. *JAMA Psychiatry* 76(6):642–651. doi:10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2019.0174 [abstract].** 17 cohort studies, 64 models, more than 14 million participants. Overall classification accuracy was "good (≥0.80 in most models)," but the PPV for suicide death was "**≤0.01 in most models**." In the authors' words, their "accuracy of predicting a future event is near 0." — [Belsher et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2019.0174)
- **McHugh CM, Corderoy A, Ryan CJ, Hickie IB, Large MM (2019). Association between suicidal ideation and suicide: meta-analyses of odds ratios, sensitivity, specificity and positive predictive value. *BJPsych Open* 5(2):e18. doi:10.1192/bjo.2018.88 [abstract; erratum doi:10.1192/bjo.2019.15].** 71 studies. Expressed SI gave a suicide OR of **3.41** (2.59–4.49; prediction interval 0.42–28.1; I² = 89.4). Sensitivity was 41% and specificity 86%. **PPV was 0.3% in non-psychiatric cohorts and 3.9% in psychiatric samples.** Even a truthful "yes" to SI is a weak test for death. — [McHugh et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjo.2018.88)
- **Pompili et al. 2016** (above): communication of intent has an OR of 4.66 but "insufficient accuracy." — [Pompili et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291716000696)

### Inferences
- **Implication for PoesIA.** A safety pause triggered by text cannot be justified as *risk prediction*. With a base rate for suicide death far below 1%, even a flag much better than any published instrument would be wrong far more often than right, and a "no flag" would not mean "safe". Louzon found 71.6% of VA suicides among "not at all" responders; Berman found deniers dying within 2 days. The defensible framing is a *needs-based, universal offer of support*: always-visible resources, a low-friction pause that does not punish. The language should not be risk stratification.
- The "near chance" result applies to single risk factors (Franklin). Clinical high-risk categorisation does better on OR and AUC but is still useless on PPV (Large, Carter). The report should not write "clinicians are no better than chance"; it should write "risk categorisation, by clinicians, scales or algorithms, has too low a PPV to guide individual decisions."
- If PoesIA ever evaluates its detector, it should report PPV and NPV at a realistic prevalence, not only AUC (Belsher's lesson).

### Gaps
- NICE NG225 (2022), which recommends against using risk assessment tools or scales to predict suicide or self-harm repetition: the NICE page returned 403, so the exact wording and recommendation number are **not seen** and not verified here.
- No data on how a text classifier of creative writing performs at predicting suicidal behaviour. Out of scope here and possibly not studied.
- Franklin is not about short-term (hours–days) prediction. Imminent-risk prediction evidence was not reviewed.

## Q5. Does asking about suicide increase risk?

### Takeaway
No. RCTs and meta-analyses consistently find no increase in SI, distress or behaviour from asking, and some find small reductions. Limits: the evidence comes from research and screening settings (questionnaires and interviews, mostly students, adolescents and research volunteers), and follow-up is short.

### Cited Findings
- **Gould MS, Marrocco FA, Kleinman M, Thomas JG, Mostkoff K, Cote J, Davies M (2005). Evaluating iatrogenic risk of youth suicide screening programs: a randomized controlled trial. *JAMA* 293(13):1635–1643. doi:10.1001/jama.293.13.1635 [abstract].** 2,342 students in 6 New York high schools, randomised by class to a survey with (n = 1,172) or without (n = 1,170) suicide questions. Distress immediately after (POMS-A 5.5 vs 5.1, p = .66) and 2 days later (4.3 vs 3.9, p = .41) did not differ, nor did SI 2 days later (4.7% vs 3.9%, p = .49). High-risk students who were asked were less distressed (depressed students, p = .01) and less suicidal (prior attempters, p = .02). — [Gould et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.293.13.1635)
- **Dazzi T, Gribble R, Wessely S, Fear NT (2014). Does asking about suicide and related behaviours induce suicidal ideation? What is the evidence? *Psychological Medicine* 44(16):3361–3363. doi:10.1017/S0033291714001299 [abstract].** A short **qualitative review**, not a meta-analysis; Blades et al. describe it as covering 13 published studies. "None found a statistically significant increase in suicidal ideation among participants asked about suicidal thoughts," and asking "may in fact reduce" SI. User citation is correct. — [Dazzi et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291714001299); study count per [Blades et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2018.07.001)
- **DeCou CR, Schumann ME (2018). On the iatrogenic risk of assessing suicidality: a meta-analysis. *Suicide and Life-Threatening Behavior* 48(5):531–543. doi:10.1111/sltb.12368 (epub 2017) [abstract].** 13 prospective articles; no significant iatrogenic effect. Blades et al. add that it found none at immediate, short-term or long-term follow-up, including among high-risk groups, but it did not examine suicidal *behaviour*. — [DeCou & Schumann 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/sltb.12368)
- **Blades CA, Stritzke WGK, Page AC, Brown JD (2018). The benefits and risks of asking research participants about suicide: a meta-analysis of the impact of exposure to suicide-related content. *Clinical Psychology Review* 64:1–12. doi:10.1016/j.cpr.2018.07.001 [full text, accepted manuscript].** 18 studies (17 published plus 1 dissertation), N = 13,192, 2000–2017. Findings:
  - SI pre→post: **g = −0.13** (95% CI −0.16 to −0.10; 4 studies, n = 3,699; I² = 0); the reduction was about twice as large in adolescents.
  - SI, exposed vs not: OR 0.973 (0.83–1.15; 7 studies, n = 7,398).
  - Suicide attempts, exposed vs not: **OR 0.714** (0.56–0.91; 4 studies, n = 5,261).
  - Distress pre→post: g = −0.09 (n.s.); immediate between-group g = −0.01; delayed g = 0.04 (n.s.).
  - **Mode moderator:** "exposing participants to suicide-related content in a one-on-one interview context led to small, significant reductions in distress over time. In contrast, participants … responding to written self-report questionnaires did not show significant changes in distress."

  Caveats: the attempt pool includes Vaiva et al. 2006, a *telephone-contact intervention* after self-poisoning, so the OR 0.714 is not pure "asking". Only 4 studies contributed to that pool. — [Blades et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2018.07.001); [accepted manuscript](https://api.research-repository.uwa.edu.au/ws/files/33499098/Full_text.pdf)
- **Polihronis C, Cloutier P, Kaur J, Skinner R, Cappelli M (2022). What's the harm in asking? A systematic review and meta-analysis on the risks of asking about suicide-related behaviors and self-harm with quality appraisal. *Archives of Suicide Research* 26(2):325–347. doi:10.1080/13811118.2020.1793857 (epub 2020) [abstract].** 17 studies rated, 8 meta-analysed (asked vs not asked). There were "no statistically significant effects of asking on SRB [suicide-related behaviour], NSSI [non-suicidal self-injury], or PD [psychological distress]." The 8 RCTs had low or unclear risk of bias, and the cohort studies were of low to moderate quality. The authors say more low-risk-of-bias RCTs are needed "to firmly conclude." **Correction to the title:** the published title is "…on the risks of asking about suicide-related behaviors and self-harm with quality appraisal" (it is not framed around *youth screening* as such). — [Polihronis et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/13811118.2020.1793857)

### Inferences
- PoesIA can ask a direct, plain question at a safety pause ("Are you having thoughts of suicide?") without evidence of iatrogenic harm. Blades' moderator suggests a supportive *interactive* exchange may lower distress more than a bare written checklist.
- How the question is worded matters more than whether it is asked: phrase it positively and openly, not "No thoughts of harming yourself?" (McCabe 2017). Allow "prefer not to say" and treat it as a signal (Podlogar & Joiner 2020).
- The evidence covers asking within research and screening protocols. It does not cover an app interrupting a creative session, which is untested.

### Gaps
- Polihronis 2022: effect estimates were not available from the abstract; full text not seen.
- No study found on the iatrogenic effect of *automated* or app-delivered suicide questions specifically. Most included studies used questionnaires or interviews delivered by researchers.
