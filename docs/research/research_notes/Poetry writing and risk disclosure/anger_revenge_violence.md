# Anger, revenge wishes, thoughts of harming others: do they predict violence, and what does writing about anger do?

Scope: evidence for the PoesIA safety-pause design question. Should a writer's anger or wish for revenge with no stated intent ("I want to make him regret what he said", "I want to make her pay") trigger a crisis pause, or only explicit intent to harm someone?

Notes compiled 2026-10-08. Verification legend used throughout:
- **[DOI ✓ / abstract seen]**: DOI resolved on Crossref (api.crossref.org) and the PubMed or publisher abstract was read. Findings are limited to what the abstract states.
- **[full text seen]**: the full document was read (official PDFs or text).
- **[DOI ✓ / abstract not seen]**: the citation was verified, but no findings from it are reported as verified.
- **[not seen]**: the source could not be opened. Any figure attributed to it is labelled secondary.

Corrections to the suggested citations:

## 1. How common are revenge and homicidal fantasies and violent ideation, and do they predict violence?

### Takeaway
Thoughts of killing or harming someone are common in ordinary people:
- A majority of students report at least one homicidal fantasy.
- 3.3% of US adults say they *seriously* thought about shooting someone in the past year.

Violent ideation raises the risk of violence at the group level, but only modestly:
- In psychiatric and offender samples the odds ratio is about 2.7 to 3.4.
- Even in these high-risk samples, fewer than 25% of people with violent ideation went on to be violent within 20 weeks.
- Structured risk tools used by professionals reach a median positive predictive value (PPV) of only 41% in high-base-rate samples.

So anger or a revenge wish expressed on its own is a weak predictor. Most people who voice such wishes are not violent.

### Cited findings

**Homicidal and revenge fantasies in non-clinical samples**
- Kenrick & Sheets (1993) ran two studies of homicidal fantasies in students:
  - A majority in both studies reported at least one homicidal fantasy.
  - Study 1: 73% of men and 66% of women. Study 2: 79% of men and 58% of women.
  - Men reported longer and more detailed fantasies. Women's recent fantasies more often involved family members.
  - **Caveat:** the publisher page returned 403. These figures come from the abstract as reproduced by an abstract index ([scholarhub.vn record](https://scholarhub.vn/publication/Homicidal-fantasies/01f4bd0b-6a1e-428f-b7b2-4c29d32decd6)) and a search-engine summary of it, not from the full text. The citation itself is verified ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0162-3095(93)90019-E)).
- Crabb (2000) asked normal undergraduates to describe a recent time they *thought about killing someone* ([abstract, Penn State Pure record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-material-culture-of-homicidal-fantasies/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1098-2337(2000)26:3%3C225::AID-AB2%3E3.0.CO;2-R)).
  - "A significant proportion of participants reported having homicidal fantasies."
  - Most fantasies "were elicited by frustrating or threatening interpersonal events", which is the same trigger profile as a poem about being wronged.
  - The abstract gives no prevalence figure.
- Buss's 91% (men) and 84% (women) "at least one vivid homicidal fantasy" figures appear only in secondary and popular sources found in search. **Not seen** in a primary source, so do not cite them as verified.

**Violent ideation and later violence (prospective designs)**
- Grisso, Davis, Vesselinov, Appelbaum & Monahan (2000), MacArthur Violence Risk Assessment Study, psychiatric inpatients followed for 20 weeks after discharge ([PubMed 10883555](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10883555/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.68.3.388)):
  - About one in three patients reported thoughts of violence while in hospital, "more than twice the proportion found among nonpatients".
  - Violent thoughts in hospital predicted violent acts only in subgroups: non-White patients, patients with substance-abuse diagnoses but no major mental disorder, patients with high symptom severity, and patients whose violent thoughts *persisted* after discharge.
  - Violent thoughts correlated with psychopathy, anger and impulsiveness.
- Persson, Sturup, Belfrage & Kristiansson (2018), Sweden, prospective, 20-week follow-up using registry, interview and records ([PubMed 29316458](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29316458/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2017.12.079)):
  - Samples: 200 forensic detainees and 390 general psychiatric patients at discharge.
  - Lifetime violent ideation: 32.5% of offenders and 35.6% of patients. Two-month prevalence: 22.5% and 21.0%.
  - Lifetime ideation predicted violence: OR = 2.65 in both samples combined, OR = 3.41 in patients.
  - "In terms of positive predictive values, fewer than 25% of those with violent ideation committed violent acts."
  - The authors' conclusion: the link holds "on a group level", but individual assessment needs additional factors.
- Furnes, Gjestad, Rypdal, Mehlum, Hart, Oedegaard & Mellesdal (2021), Norway, 1,737 consecutive acute psychiatric admissions, cross-sectional ([PubMed 33368501](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33368501/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/sltb.12726)):
  - Point prevalence: 51.9% suicidal ideation and 19.8% violent ideation.
  - 12.3% had both, which was "significantly greater than expected by chance".
  - Violent ideation was associated with male sex, younger age, suicidal ideation, and mood or neurotic disorders (not psychotic disorders).

**General population: thoughts of shooting others**
- Hicks & Ilgen (2026), *JAMA Network Open* 9(3):e260734, cross-sectional, address-based sample of 7,034 US adults weighted to the July 2025 Current Population Survey ([PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13343161/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.0734)):
  - 3.3% had *seriously* thought about shooting another person in the past 12 months (about 8.7 million adults). Lifetime prevalence was 7.3%.
  - 1.6% thought about acquiring a firearm for this purpose. 0.6% brought a gun to a location intending to shoot someone.
  - Correlates: younger age, male sex, Black race, urban residence and the Midwest. Gun ownership was not significantly associated.
  - The paper does not measure whether these thoughts led to violence.
  - Limitations: online, English-only survey; possible misreading of the questions.
  - Source note: this paper was read through a WebFetch summary of the PMC full text.

**Intrusive harm thoughts versus rehearsed "aggressive scripts"**
- Fernandez, Daffern, Moulding & Nedeljkovic (2023), nonclinical sample of 412 adults, cross-sectional self-report ([PubMed 36408970](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36408970/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ab.22061)):
  - "Experiencing a thought about harming or injuring another person is commonly reported by the general population."
  - Aggressive script rehearsal was predicted by violence-supportive beliefs and by how often the person ruminates on anger. Script rehearsal, anger rumination and violence-supportive beliefs predicted a history of aggression.
  - Aggressive *intrusive* thoughts were predicted by obsessive beliefs and were ego-dystonic.
  - Script rehearsal, not having the thought at all, is the feature linked to aggression history.

**Anger as a pathway in clinical samples**
- Ullrich, Keers & Coid (2014), reanalysis of the MacArthur data, 1,136 discharged civil psychiatric inpatients ([PubMed 24048345](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24048345/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/schbul/sbt126)):
  - Persecutory and control delusions predicted serious violence only when measured close in time to it (adjusted ORs about 1.6 to 1.9).
  - "Anger due to delusions is the key factor in this pathway." Adding anger to the model attenuated the effects of the delusions.
- Swanson, Sampson, Petukhova, Zaslavsky, Appelbaum, Swartz & Kessler (2015), NCS-R, US nationally representative ([PubMed 25850688](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25850688/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/bsl.2172)):
  - 8.9% of US adults self-report impulsive angry behaviour and have a gun at home. 1.5% have that behaviour and carry a gun outside the home.
  - The authors propose behaviour-based restrictions (violent misdemeanours, repeat DUIs) as the more effective policy route.

**Accuracy of professional risk prediction**
- Fazel, Singh, Doll & Grann (2012), BMJ 345:e4692, systematic review and meta-analysis ([PubMed 22833604](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22833604/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.e4692)):
  - 73 samples, 24,847 participants, 13 countries. 23.7% offended over a mean of 49.6 months.
  - Violence-risk tools: median PPV 41% (IQR 27–60%), median NPV 91% (81–95%). Number needed to detain = 2; number safely discharged = 10.
  - The tools identify low-risk people well. Their use "as sole determinants of detention, sentencing, and release is not supported".

**Revenge and rumination**
- Carlsmith, Wilson & Gilbert (2008), three experiments ([PubMed 19025285](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19025285/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0012165)):
  - People who punish an offender "continue to ruminate about the offender", whereas non-punishers "move on".
  - People mispredict the hedonic payoff of revenge.
- McCullough, Bellah, Kilpatrick & Johnson (2001), "Vengefulness: relationships with forgiveness, rumination, well-being, and the Big Five" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167201275008)). **[DOI ✓ / abstract not seen]:** no findings reported.
- Horowitz (2007), "Understanding and ameliorating revenge fantasies in psychotherapy", a clinical commentary ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1176/ajp.2007.164.1.24)). **[DOI ✓ / text not seen]:** no findings reported.

### Inferences
- **Base rates make a pause on anger alone mostly false positives.** Fantasies about killing someone who wronged you are reported by most students (Kenrick & Sheets, as reported; Crabb). Even the far stronger cue "seriously thought about shooting someone" runs at 3.3% a year in US adults. Serious violence is much rarer than either.
- **The poetry-app PPV will be well below 25%.** That figure is a ceiling from psychiatric samples with high base rates. A general-population writing app, by Bayes, will have a much lower PPV for "revenge wish without intent", probably far below 41% and below 25%.
- **What the predictive studies point to.** Ideation predicts violence when it is persistent (Grisso), rehearsed as a script with violence-supportive beliefs (Fernandez), or combined with substance misuse, high symptom load or a history of aggression. These are signals of *persistence, rehearsal and capability*, not anger as such.
- **Watch for co-occurrence with suicidal content.** Suicidal and violent ideation co-occur more than chance (Furnes: 12.3% of acute admissions). A text that mixes a revenge wish with suicidal or last-resort content deserves more weight than either alone. This links to the suicide side of the safety pause.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests whether *written* revenge wishes in a non-clinical population (social media, diaries, creative writing) predict later violence. All the predictive data come from psychiatric or forensic follow-ups, or from retrospective attacker studies (section 2).
- No pooled meta-analytic estimate of the violent-ideation-to-violence link in the general population was found.
- Kenrick & Sheets figures are not confirmed against the full text (publisher 403). Buss's figures are not seen.
- Studies of homicidal ideation in emergency-department patients turned up only paediatric service reports, nothing with predictive data. Asnis et al. on psychiatric outpatients (1994) was not located through PubMed title search. **Not seen.**

## 2. Leakage and threat assessment: how do the frameworks separate expressed anger from targeted intent?

### Takeaway
Behavioural threat assessment (US Secret Service NTAC, Meloy and colleagues) states plainly that **making a threat is not the same as posing one**:
- Most people who make threats do not pose one.
- Most attackers never directly threaten their target.

The signals these frameworks act on are *pathway* behaviours rather than anger:
- leakage of *intent* to third parties
- fixation on a target
- research and planning, and acquiring weapons
- identification with attackers
- last-resort or "final act" communications

Grievance is the commonest motive among attackers (about half), but it is a context, not a trigger on its own. NTAC also warns *against* waiting for a specific threat (target, place, time) *when other warning signs are present*.

### Cited findings

**Making versus posing a threat**
- Fein, Vossekuil & Holden (1995, NIJ *Research in Action*, NCJ 155000) ([official text, OJP](https://www.ojp.gov/txtfiles/threat.txt); full text seen):
  - "Individuals utter threats for many reasons, only some of which involve intention or capacity to commit a violent act. However, a person can present a grave threat without articulating it."
  - "Some persons who make threats ultimately pose threats. Many persons who make threats do not pose threats. Some persons who pose threats never make threats."
- Fein & Vossekuil (2000), *Protective Intelligence and Threat Assessment Investigations: A Guide* (NIJ/USSS) ([official text, OJP](https://www.ojp.gov/txtfiles1/nij/179981.txt); full text seen). This guide reports the Exceptional Case Study Project (ECSP): all 83 people who attacked or came close to attacking US public figures, 1949–1990s.
  - Myth 3 is named as "The persons most likely to carry out attacks are those who make direct threats." The guide's answer: "Persons who pose an actual threat often do not make threats, especially direct threats."
  - "None of the 43 assassins and attackers communicated a direct threat to the target before their attack."
  - "Fewer than one-tenth of all 83 attackers and near-lethal approachers communicated a direct threat to the target or to a law enforcement agency."
  - "Two-thirds ... were known to have spoken or written in a manner suggesting that they were considering an attack ... or they wrote down their ideas in journals or diaries." This is leakage, and it can include private writing.
  - "Almost all had histories of grievances and resentments." "Most had histories of explosive, angry behavior, but only half ... had histories of physically violent behavior."
  - Attack-related behaviours: choosing a target, learning where the target will be, securing a weapon, surveying security, developing a plan, and considering escape.
- Fein & Vossekuil (1999), the peer-reviewed ECSP paper ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1520/JFS14457J); abstract seen): "In every case, the attack or near-attack was the end result of an understandable, and often discernible, process of thinking and action."

**Leakage and warning behaviours**
- Meloy & O'Toole (2011) ([PubMed 21710573](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21710573/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/bsl.986)):
  - Definition: leakage is "the communication to a third party of an intent to do harm to a target". The channels include "letters, diaries, journals, blogs, videos on the internet, emails".
  - Leakage "typically infers a preoccupation with the target, and may signal the research, planning, and implementation of an attack".
  - It "occurs in a majority of cases" of public-figure attacks, adult and adolescent mass murders and school shootings, but these are "very low-frequency" events.
  - The evidence is nomothetic, from attacker samples without a comparison group, plus case studies.
- Meloy, Hoffmann, Guldimann & James (2012) ([PubMed 22556034](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22556034/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/bsl.999)) propose eight warning behaviours, which are "acute, dynamic, and particularly toxic changes in patterns of behavior":
  - pathway
  - fixation
  - identification
  - novel aggression
  - energy burst
  - leakage
  - directly communicated threat
  - last resort

**NTAC, *Mass Attacks in Public Spaces: 2016–2020* (2023)** ([official PDF](https://www.secretservice.gov/sites/default/files/reports/2023-01/usss-ntac-maps-2016-2020.pdf); full text seen). Retrospective, open-source study of 173 attacks and 180 attackers (three or more people harmed):
- Grievances motivated 51% of attacks (n = 88). Attackers were "retaliating for perceived wrongs related to personal, domestic, or workplace issues".
- 66% (119/180) made prior threatening or otherwise concerning communications.
- Of those 119, 64% directly or indirectly threatened to harm others, and 34% (n = 41) threatened the eventual target.
- Of those 41, most said they would kill the target (n = 30), but few said where (n = 16) or when (n = 14).
- 76% exhibited concerning behaviours or communications. For 57%, observers feared for someone's safety.
- "Communities should not wait for a direct and specific threat before taking action ... Such specificity ... should not be viewed as a threshold for taking preventive action when other warning signs of violence are present."
- "Many of the pre-attack behaviors described in this report are not suspicious on their own ... Most individuals who exhibit these types of behaviors will not commit acts of targeted violence ... there is no profile."
- Nearly one-quarter conveyed concerning communications online.
- 37% (44/119) made a concerning communication on the day of the attack. NTAC lists "final communications" or final acts as warranting immediate intervention.

### Inferences
- **The frameworks treat grievance as context, not a trigger.** In threat assessment, "I want to make her pay" is a grievance statement. Grievance is the most common motive among attackers, but it is also ubiquitous among people who never attack. Neither the ECSP nor NTAC can give a PPV, because both studied only attackers and had no comparison group.
- **What would move a text toward a pause** (an analogue of the frameworks' signals, not a validated detector):
  - a specific, identifiable target together with means, plan, time or place
  - weapon acquisition or rehearsal
  - fixation (persistent, escalating preoccupation with one person)
  - identification with previous attackers
  - "last resort" or "nothing left to lose" framing
  - goodbye or final-act language
  - co-occurring suicidal statements
- **Don't over-correct.** NTAC's warning against waiting for specificity applies *when other warning signs are present*. It argues against requiring target, place and time before acting. It does not argue for treating every grievance as a crisis.
- **Diaries and private writing are a known leakage channel** (ECSP; Meloy & O'Toole). A poetry app is plausibly such a channel. That supports having *some* response to explicit intent or attack-related content in poems. It does not support a pause on anger alone.

### Gaps
- No prospective study was found that measures the PPV of "leakage" in a general population. All the leakage literature samples attackers.
- Dietz et al. (1991) on threatening letters to celebrities and Congress (letter-writing not associated with approach) was not located in PubMed by title. **Not seen**, so it is not reported.
- The 2018 FBI *Pre-Attack Behaviors of Active Shooters* report and the NTAC school-attack reports were not opened in this pass. **Not seen.**

## 3. Writing about anger: does it reduce or increase aggression?

### Takeaway
It depends on the *kind* of writing.

**Increases anger and aggression:**
- venting or ruminating on the offender (anger and aggression go up in experiments)
- writing on rant sites (negative mood shifts)

**Reduces anger or aggression:**
- arousal-lowering strategies
- distraction
- self-distancing
- benefit-finding or forgiveness-oriented writing
- structured expressive writing

The effects of the helpful strategies are small to moderate.

Imagining violent revenge did not raise aggressive emotions in one small lab study, but it did not help either.

### Cited findings

**Venting and rumination**
- Bushman (2002), lab experiment ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167202289002); abstract via Crossref):
  - Design: angered participants hit a punching bag while thinking about the provoker (rumination), or while thinking about fitness (distraction); a third group had no bag.
  - The rumination group felt angrier and was the most aggressive (noise blasts at the provoker), followed by distraction, then control.
  - "Doing nothing at all was more effective than venting anger."
- Bushman, Baumeister & Stack (1999), two experiments ([PubMed 10101875](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10101875/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.76.3.367)): reading a pro-catharsis message and then hitting a bag led to *more* subsequent lab aggression.
- Bushman, Bonacci, Pedersen, Vasquez & Miller (2005), three studies ([PubMed 15982116](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15982116/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.88.6.969)):
  - Ruminating about a provocation for 25 minutes, or over 8 hours, increased displaced aggression after a minor trigger.
  - Negative affect from the provocation predicted aggression "only among those who ruminated".

**Meta-analytic evidence**
- Kjærvik & Bushman (2024) ([PubMed 38518585](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38518585/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2024.102414)):
  - 154 studies, 184 samples, N = 10,189.
  - Arousal-decreasing activities (breathing, mindfulness, meditation) reduced anger and aggression: g = −0.63 [−0.82, −0.43]. This held across delivery modes "including digital platforms" and in offenders and non-offenders.
  - Arousal-increasing activities (hitting a bag, jogging) had no effect: g = −0.02 [−0.13, 0.09].
  - The abstract does not classify *writing*.

**Writing on rant sites**
- Martin, Coyier, VanSistine & Schroeder (2013), two studies ([PubMed 23249241](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23249241/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1089/cyber.2012.0130)):
  - Study 1, a survey: rant-site visitors feel relaxed immediately after posting, but "experience more anger than most and express their anger in maladaptive ways".
  - Study 2, an experiment: "reading and writing rants were associated with negative shifts in mood" for most participants.

**Forgiveness and benefit-finding**
- McCullough, Root & Cohen (2006), RCT, N = 304, three 20-minute writing tasks ([PubMed 17032093](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17032093/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.74.5.887)):
  - Writing about the *benefits* arising from a transgression increased forgiveness relative to writing about its traumatic features or a control topic.
  - The trauma-writing and control groups did not differ, so venting the hurt did not increase forgiveness.
- Wade, Hoyt, Kidwell & Worthington (2014), meta-analysis of 54 reports ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=10.1037%2Fa0035268); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035268)):
  - k = 53, N = 2,323 at post-treatment.
  - Explicit forgiveness interventions versus no treatment: Δ+ = 0.56 [0.43, 0.68]. Versus alternative treatments: 0.45 [0.21, 0.69].
  - Forgiveness treatments also improved depression, anxiety and hope relative to no treatment.
  - These are professional interventions, not self-directed writing.

**Expressive writing trials with anger or aggression outcomes**
- Kliewer, Lepore, Farrell, Allison, Meyer, Sullivan & Greene (2011), school-based cluster RCT ([PubMed 21916688](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21916688/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/15374416.2011.597092)):
  - 17 classrooms, 258 seventh-graders in high-violence US neighbourhoods; eight writing sessions about experiencing or witnessing violence.
  - At 2 months, standard expressive writing lowered teacher-rated aggression and emotional lability versus control (d = −0.48).
  - Effects were stronger at higher exposure to community violence. The abstract reports no 6-month effect.
- Sayer, Noorbaloochi, Frazier, Pennebaker, Orazem, Schnurr, Murdoch, Carlson, Gravely & Litz (2015), online RCT ([PubMed 26467326](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26467326/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/jts.22047)):
  - N = 1,292 Iraq and Afghanistan veterans with reintegration difficulty.
  - At 6 months, expressive writing reduced anger (and distress and physical complaints) more than factual writing (ds = 0.13–0.20) and more than no writing (ds = 0.22–0.35).
  - "Small effect sizes".

**Disposing of written anger**
- Kanaya & Kawai (2024), two lab experiments in Japan ([PubMed 38594343](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38594343/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-57916-z)):
  - Provoked participants wrote down their thoughts about the provocation.
  - Those who threw the paper away or shredded it returned to baseline anger. Those who kept it remained above baseline.
  - The abstract does not give sample sizes. There was no aggression outcome.

**Imagined violent revenge**
- Seebauer, Froß, Dubaschny, Schönberger & Jacob (2014), within-subject experiment, 46 healthy adults, imagery rescripting after trauma film clips ([PubMed 23911715](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23911715/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbtep.2013.07.003)):
  - Rescripting with violent revenge did not increase aggressive emotions compared with non-violent rescripting.
  - Safe-place imagery worked best. Violent revenge imagery "has no added value".
  - Healthy sample only; possible cross-over effects.

**Not read**
- Mischkowski, Kross & Bushman (2012) on self-distancing "in the heat of the moment" and aggression ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2012.03.012)). **[DOI ✓ / abstract not seen]:** no findings reported.
- Frattaroli (2006), meta-analysis of experimental disclosure, *Psychological Bulletin* 132(6), 823–865 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.6.823)). **[DOI ✓ / abstract not seen].**

### Inferences
- **For product design, writing is neither inherently cathartic nor inherently dangerous.** Writing that rehearses the grievance and the offender (venting, ranting, ruminating) tends to *maintain or raise* anger. Writing that adds distance, meaning-making or benefit-finding, or that "lets go" (disposal), tends to *lower* it.
- **A pause does not deliver the helpful strategy.** The helpful strategies (distance, reappraisal, release) are better delivered as optional, non-alarming writing prompts in the flow than by stopping the writer.
- **Example prompts** (an inference, not a tested intervention):
  - write the poem from a later vantage point
  - write what you'd want to feel a year from now
  - write the poem and then "release" it
  - a breath or arousal-lowering pause offered, not imposed
- **Don't let the app become a rumination loop.** Repeatedly feeding a revenge poem back to the writer, iterating it or amplifying it, resembles the rumination conditions that increased aggression in the lab.

### Gaps
- No trial was found that tests creative *poetry* writing about anger or revenge with an aggression outcome.
- Lab aggression measures (noise blasts) are proxies. Their link to real-world violence is indirect.
- Kanaya & Kawai's sample size and any independent replication were not checked.
- Revenge-specific writing interventions with *behavioural* outcomes were not found. McCullough 2006 measured forgiveness motivations (TRIM), not behaviour.

## 4. Context: how platforms and the law separate violent ideation or venting from threats (non-scholarly)

### Takeaway
- **US law (*Counterman v. Colorado*, 2023)** treats a "true threat" as a *serious expression* of intent to commit unlawful violence, distinguished from "jests, 'hyperbole', or other statements that when taken in context do not convey a real possibility that violence will follow". It also requires at least recklessness by the speaker.
- **Meta's moderation policy** is deliberately broader. It removes "aspirational" and "conditional" statements and "expressions of hope" for violence against private persons, regardless of credibility.

Removing content from a public platform (protecting a third party who reads it) is a different job from a crisis response to a private writer.

### Cited findings
- *Counterman v. Colorado* (2023), U.S. Supreme Court, No. 22-138 ([official slip opinion](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/22-138_43j7.pdf); full text seen):
  - "The 'true' in that term distinguishes what is at issue from jests, 'hyperbole,' or other statements that when taken in context do not convey a real possibility that violence will follow (say, 'I am going to kill you for showing up late')."
  - True threats are "'serious expression[s]' conveying that a speaker means to 'commit an act of unlawful violence.'"
  - Holding: "a mental state of recklessness is sufficient. The State must show that the defendant consciously disregarded a substantial risk that his communications would be viewed as threatening violence."
- Meta, Community Standards, *Violence and Incitement* ([official page](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/community-standards/violence-incitement/); text retrieved 2026-10-08 through a text-rendering proxy, so wording may change):
  - "Threats of violence are statements or visuals representing an intention, aspiration, or call for violence against a target, and threats can be expressed in various types of statements such as statements of intent, calls for action, advocacy, expressions of hope, aspirational statements and conditional statements."
  - Mid-severity threats against public figures are removed "when credible". Against other targets they are removed "regardless of credibility".
  - Private adults are additionally protected from threats of "low-severity violence".
- X's violent-speech policy page ([help.x.com](https://help.x.com/en/rules-and-policies/violent-speech)) returned 403. **Not seen.**

### Inferences
- **Two thresholds, two different jobs.**
  - Legal threat doctrine and threat assessment ask, in context, whether a statement is a serious expression of intent, and lean on the speaker's other behaviour.
  - Platform moderation removes even aspirational or conditional wishes of harm toward private people. That protects the *target* audience on a public feed.
  - PoesIA's poems are private writing, not posts read by the target. The relevant analogue for a crisis pause is threat assessment, not content removal.
  - If PoesIA ever *publishes* or shares poems, a separate moderation layer at a Meta-like threshold could apply to publication only.
- **Synthesis for the design question.** This is an inference across sections 1–4, not a finding from any single source. The evidence supports *not* pausing on anger or a revenge wish alone ("make him regret it", "make her pay"):
  - These statements are common and have a very low PPV.
  - Threat-assessment practice separates grievance from intent.
  - Interrupting expression is not needed to prevent harm; structured or reappraisal writing reduces anger.
- **Pause, or escalate to a safety check, when any of these appears** (the threat-assessment signals and the NTAC "final act" category):
  - an explicit statement of intent to harm a person
  - a plan, means, weapon, time or place
  - an identifiable target together with preparatory or fixation language
  - identification with attackers
  - last-resort or final-act or goodbye content
  - violent intent co-occurring with suicidal content
- **For anger or revenge without those markers, a lighter-touch response fits the evidence better:**
  - continue writing
  - optionally offer distancing, reappraisal or release prompts
  - avoid amplification loops
  - possibly log for persistence or escalation over sessions, since persistence was the predictive feature in Grisso 2000. Any such logging has its own privacy trade-off.

### Gaps
- No published evaluation was found of crisis-pause thresholds for violent ideation in non-clinical creative or journaling apps. This is grey territory with no scholarly evidence located.
- Platform policy texts change over time. The quotes above are as retrieved on 2026-10-08.
