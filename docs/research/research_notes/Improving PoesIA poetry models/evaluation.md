## 1. Automatic poetry metrics validated against humans, with correlation numbers

### Takeaway
The only poetry study with a per-metric correlation table against expert overall impression is Mareček et al. 2025 (Czech, 100 poems). In it, semantics and syntax dominate: human semantics r = 0.90 and human syntax r = 0.87 with overall impression. Human-rated metre (0.16) and rhyme (0.28) barely matter. Statistical metre and rhyme detectors match human *formal* judgements (0.77 and 0.48) but not overall impression (0.10 and −0.11). The best automatic predictors were an LLM "semantics" score (0.59) and the share of words found in a lexicon (0.54). Creativity Index, perplexity and diversity indices separate humans from LLMs at the group level. None has been validated against expert ratings of individual poems.

### Cited Findings
**Mareček, Motalík Hodková, Musil & Rosa, "Evaluation of Generated Poetry", Eval4NLP 2025 (full text read)**
- **Data.** 100 Czech poems, 20 from each of five sources: the Corpus of Czech Verse (human), gpt-4o-mini, and three versions of their LoRA-tuned Llama 3.1. Free verse was excluded. — [ACL 2025.eval4nlp-1.9](https://aclanthology.org/2025.eval4nlp-1.9)
- **Raters.** Three paid experts: a linguist, a versologist and a literary expert. Only *semantics* was rated by all three. Every other human metric comes from one expert. The linguist "was incapable" of rating metre, so the versologist redid it. — [same](https://aclanthology.org/2025.eval4nlp-1.9)
- **Inter-annotator reliability for semantics.** Pairwise correlations between experts were 0.53, 0.71 and 0.75. — [same](https://aclanthology.org/2025.eval4nlp-1.9)
- **Correlation with human overall impression (Pearson, Table 2):**
  - Human-rated metrics: semantics 0.90, syntax 0.87, known words 0.67, rhyming 0.28, metre 0.16.
  - LLM-rated metrics (gpt-4o-mini, temperature 0, 0–10 scale): semantics 0.59, syntax 0.56, metre 0.64, rhyming 0.61.
  - Statistical metrics: known words 0.54, metre (the Květa tool) 0.10, rhyming (RhymeTagger) −0.11.
  - Combining LLM semantics × known words gave 0.62. — [same](https://aclanthology.org/2025.eval4nlp-1.9)
- **The LLM's formal ratings are a halo, not form judgement.**
  - The LLM's metre and rhyme scores correlate with the human metre and rhyme ratings at only 0.17 and 0.21.
  - All the LLM metrics correlate with each other at 0.71–0.80 (Table 4).
  - The model "resorts to judging meaningfulness even when prompted to judge metre or rhyming".
  - Chain-of-thought produced correct theory but "mostly incorrect metre and rhyme scheme labels".
  - Few-shot and chain-of-thought prompts gave no notable improvement. — [same](https://aclanthology.org/2025.eval4nlp-1.9)
- **Statistical form tools track the human form ratings but not quality.** Metre correlates at 0.77 and rhyme at 0.48 with the corresponding human ratings. Yet "even professional human evaluators do not care much about the metre and rhyming in generated poetry". — [same](https://aclanthology.org/2025.eval4nlp-1.9)
- **The judge's validity collapses on good poems (range restriction).** Correlation of LLM semantics with human semantics:
  - 0.69 on the generated poems.
  - 0.26 on the 20 human poems alone.
  - Known words: 0.74 vs 0.37. Rhyming: 0.48 vs −0.04 (Table 3). — [same](https://aclanthology.org/2025.eval4nlp-1.9)
- **Self-preference.** gpt-4o-mini "favors its own results over all other systems (including human-written poems) in all LLM-based metrics", which the human ratings do not support. The authors conclude that LLM semantics "can be used to compare the quality of multiple individual poems generated by one system, but cannot reliably compare" the judge's own poems with other systems. — [same](https://aclanthology.org/2025.eval4nlp-1.9)
- **The generators traded meaning for form.** The authors' own models "often generated too many non-existent and mostly nonsensical words" while trying to meet metre and rhyme ("clearly overtuned for formal quality ... at the cost of their meaningfulness"). Known-words still correlated 0.50 with overall impression within the human poems alone. — [same](https://aclanthology.org/2025.eval4nlp-1.9)
- **Experts tolerate formal deviation.** The annotators "were reluctant to rate a poem poorly if it was not formally perfect", because poets break the rules on purpose. — [same](https://aclanthology.org/2025.eval4nlp-1.9)

**Creativity Index (Lu, Sclar, Hallinan, … Choi; arXiv 2410.04265, v2 January 2025; no venue on the arXiv page)**
- **Method.** L-uniqueness is the share of words not covered by any n-gram of length ≥ L found in a reference corpus: RedPajama, about 900 M documents, searched with Infini-gram. Word Mover's Distance on content words is used for near-verbatim matches. The index sums L-uniqueness over n ≥ L. — [arXiv 2410.04265](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.04265)
- **Poetry result.** Human poems from PoemHunter score 31.1 % higher than LLM poems (verbatim matching, Mann-Whitney p = 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁵, N = 600). Across all domains, professional authors score 66.2 % higher than LLMs, and alignment lowers LLM scores by 30.1 %. — [same](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.04265)
- **No validation against humans.** The paper reports no human creativity or quality ratings and no correlation with them. The authors' own caveats: results depend on the reference corpus, and overlap does not prove memorisation. — [same](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.04265)

**Rethinking Creativity Evaluation (Li-Chun Lu, Miri Liu, Pin Chun Lu, Yufei Tian, Shao-Hua Sun, Nanyun Peng; EACL 2026)**
- They tested four metrics (perplexity, LLM-as-judge, Creativity Index, syntactic templates) on human-aligned creative vs uncreative sets in three domains, including creative writing.
- Perplexity "reflects fluency rather than novelty". The LLM judge is inconsistent under small prompt changes and shows label bias. The Creativity Index "mostly captures lexical diversity" and is sensitive to implementation details. The metrics disagree on individual items.
- Source: abstract and search summary; the full text was not read. — [ACL 2026.eacl-long.297](https://aclanthology.org/2026.eacl-long.297); [arXiv 2508.05470](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.05470)

**Perplexity**
- No study found validates perplexity against human fluency or quality ratings of poems. Chinese poetry papers use it as a fluency proxy and say BLEU and perplexity "are not perfect metrics". — search results incl. [arXiv 2005.01556](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.01556) (snippet only)
- Surprisal-based acceptability (SLOR on GPT-2 Small) predicted acceptability overall but "left substantial variance unexplained". — Lu, Merchan, Wang & Degen, SCiL 2024, [UMass open publishing](https://openpublishing.library.umass.edu/scil/article/id/2125) (snippet only)

**BERTScore**
- I found no poetry study validating BERTScore against human judgement. BERTScore needs a reference text. In poetry it has been used only as a homogenisation or diversity measure, which the earlier embeddings note covers (Shaib et al.). This is a gap, not evidence that it fails.

**Earlier findings that still stand** (details in the earlier notes):
- POEMetric judge vs 7 experts on 58 poems: QWK 0.361, Spearman 0.378.
- DSI vs human creativity ratings of short narratives: r up to about 0.85.
- Chen et al. 2024: no model reaches human-level within-sample diversity.

### Inferences
- **Mareček supports PoesIA's design choice.** Meaning beats form for overall impression. A metre counter at 93.5 % exact is a valid *form gate* (Mareček's statistical metre tool reached 0.77 against a versologist), but form scores will not rank poems. Use them as pass/fail gates with tolerance, never as quality signals.
- **A "known words" or lexicon gate is the cheapest validated sense proxy** (r 0.54, and 0.50 within human poems). For PoesIA: the share of tokens absent from a large inflected lexicon per language, after removing proper names. Hunspell or Wiktionary-derived lists would serve es, en, it, de and fr. It catches the specific failure where a model invents words to hit rhyme or metre.
- **PoesIA's 70B verdict shows the same range restriction as Mareček's Table 3.** It separates real from shuffled lines, just as gpt-4o-mini separated nonsense from sense on generated poems (r 0.69). It cannot rank competent lines, just as gpt-4o-mini fell to r 0.26 within human poems. That is a known property of LLM sense judges, not a bug in the prompt.
- **Creativity Index and perplexity need the same "band, not higher-is-better" treatment** the earlier note applied to lexical diversity. Each separates groups of poems, and none is validated on single poems. The Creativity Index also needs a 900 M-document index per language, which PoesIA does not have.

### Gaps
- No per-metric correlation with expert ratings exists for Spanish, Italian, German or French poems. Mareček is the only such table, and it covers Czech rhymed and metrical verse.
- No study validates perplexity or BERTScore against human judgement on poems.
- Most of Mareček's human metrics rest on one expert. Only semantics has three raters, and the sample is 100 poems.

## 2. LLM-as-judge for creative writing: agreement, pairwise vs absolute, self-preference with same-family judges, rubrics, small trained judges

### Takeaway
New evidence since the earlier note points one way:
- An LLM judge's validity depends on the quality range it sees (Mareček).
- Self-preference may be driven by **low perplexity / familiarity** (Wataoka) or by **authorship labels** (Chae et al. 2026). It extends to **similar models**, not only to the judge itself (Goel et al.).

A same-family judge is therefore the worst case. The documented mitigations that remain open to PoesIA are:
- blind, label-free prompts;
- perplexity-matched controls;
- checklist decomposition (TICK, CheckEval);
- a small judge fine-tuned on the owner's labels;
- **statistical debiasing with a few human labels**, through prediction-powered inference.

Debiasing has a hard ceiling: when the judge is no more accurate than the model it evaluates, it can at most halve the human labels needed (Dorner et al., ICLR 2025).

### Cited Findings
**Self-preference: mechanism and family effects**
- **Perplexity as the mechanism.** Wataoka, Takahashi & Ri (arXiv 2410.21819): LLM judges "assign significantly higher evaluations to outputs with lower perplexity than human evaluators, regardless of whether the outputs were self-generated". The authors conclude "the essence of the bias lies in perplexity". — [arXiv 2410.21819](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21819)
- **Labels as the mechanism.** Chae et al., "Self- and Other-Labels Induce Bidirectional Bias in LLM Judges" (arXiv 2608.18091, listed as EMNLP Findings 2026):
  - 10 judge LLMs scored sets of narrative constraints.
  - Under blind evaluation, self-preference "disappears"; a small opposite effect remains.
  - Self- and other-labels alone inflate or deflate scores whatever the true source.
  - This contradicts the familiarity account in their setting. The task is constraint selection, not free verse. — [arXiv 2608.18091](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18091)
- **Similarity, not only identity.** Goel et al., "Great Models Think Alike and this Undermines AI Oversight" (ICML 2025): with CAPA (chance-adjusted agreement on mistakes), LLM-judge scores are "biased towards more similar models", controlling for capability. — [arXiv 2502.04313](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04313)
- **Some self-preference is earned.** Chen, Wei, Zhu, Feng & Meng (arXiv 2504.03846), on verifiable tasks: much of stronger models' self-preference coincides with objectively better answers. "Harmful" self-preference is larger when the strong model is wrong. Long chain-of-thought before the verdict reduces the harmful part. Peer review status not seen. — [arXiv 2504.03846](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.03846)
- **Self-preference in poetry specifically.** gpt-4o-mini rated its own Czech poems above human poems on every LLM metric. — [Mareček et al. 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.eval4nlp-1.9)

**Limits and statistics of debiasing with few human labels**
- **The "twice the data" bound.** Dorner, Nastl & Hardt, ICLR 2025 oral: "when the judge is no more accurate than the evaluated model, no debiasing method can decrease the required amount of ground truth labels by more than half". In practice the savings were "even more modest than what our theoretical limit suggests". — [arXiv 2410.13341](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13341)
- **Prediction-powered inference (PPI).** It combines a small labelled set with many model predictions. The confidence intervals stay valid whatever the predictor, and a more accurate predictor gives narrower intervals. — Angelopoulos, Bates, Fannjiang, Jordan & Zrnic, *Science* 382:669–674 (2023), [arXiv 2301.09633](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09633)
- **AutoEval Done Right.** PPI applied to model evaluation, covering metrics and **pairwise comparisons**. It reports up to 50 % larger effective human sample sizes with GPT-4 as the judge. — Boyeau, Angelopoulos, Yosef, Malik & Jordan, [arXiv 2403.07008](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07008)
- **AutoEval can backfire.** A weak autoevaluator can make sample efficiency *worse* than human labels alone. R-AutoEval+ adapts how much weight it gives the judge. — Park, Zecchin & Simeone, NeurIPS 2025, [arXiv 2505.18659](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18659)
- **Prediction-powered ranking.**
  - A small set of human pairwise comparisons plus many LLM comparisons yields a *rank-set* per model, with coverage guaranteed asymptotically.
  - No assumption is made about how well the judge aligns with humans.
  - On Chatbot Arena, rankings built from strong-LLM comparisons alone "are often inconsistent" with human preferences.
  - Chatzi, Straitouri, Thejaswi & Gomez-Rodriguez, NeurIPS 2024. — [arXiv 2402.17826](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17826)

**Rubric design**
- **TICK (checklists).** Cook et al. 2024: LLM-generated yes/no checklists per instruction raised exact judge–human agreement from 46.4 % to 52.2 %. Giving the checklists to human annotators raised their agreement from 0.194 to 0.256 on WildBench. Not tested on creative writing. — [arXiv 2410.03608](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03608)
- **CheckEval.** Lee et al. 2024 split each criterion into Boolean sub-questions. They report stronger correlation with humans and better agreement across 12 evaluator models, validated on SummEval (summaries), not on creative text. — [arXiv 2403.18771](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.18771)
- **Simple prompts did as well as elaborate ones for poetry.** Few-shot and chain-of-thought prompts did not improve gpt-4o-mini's poetry ratings. — [Mareček et al. 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.eval4nlp-1.9)
- **LLM judges are prompt-fragile.** They are "inconsistent under small prompt changes" and show label bias. — [Lu et al., EACL 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.eacl-long.297)

**Writer-derived defect taxonomies (a rubric source)**
- Chakrabarty, Laban & Wu (CHI 2025): professional writers edited LLM creative text. Their agreed complaints condensed into a 7-category taxonomy of idiosyncrasies, such as clichés and unnecessary exposition. Categories were kept only if at least 4 participants raised them.
- The LAMP corpus holds 1,057 paragraphs edited by writers.
- No model family (GPT-4o, Claude-3.5-Sonnet, Llama-3.1-70B) clearly beat the others. Automatic edits improved rankings, but expert edits stayed best. — [arXiv 2409.14509](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14509)

**Small trained judges**
- **M-Prometheus.** Open multilingual judges of 3B–14B, fine-tuned from Qwen2.5-Instruct.
  - They beat open judges on multilingual reward benchmarks in 20+ languages and on literary-translation evaluation in 4 language pairs.
  - Ablations: the backbone choice and *natively* multilingual (not translated) feedback data matter most.
  - The training data were generated with Claude-3.5-Sonnet.
  - Unbabel and CMU, arXiv 2504.04953; abstract-level only. — [arXiv 2504.04953](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.04953)
- **Earlier evidence for trained judges.** LitBench reward models reached 78 % vs 73 % zero-shot. WQRM reached 74 % where frontier models scored near chance. Details are in the earlier note.

### Inferences
- **Why the 70B passes the 8B's lines as often as poets' lines (two hypotheses to test, not conclusions):**
  - (a) *Range restriction* (Mareček). The verdict separates sense from nonsense but not good from competent.
  - (b) *Familiarity* (Wataoka). Apertus-8B lines have low perplexity under Apertus-70B, which raises the pass rate.

  Test (b) by logging the 70B's per-token log-probability of each line. Then compare pass rates in perplexity-matched bins of poet lines vs 8B lines. If the 8B advantage disappears within the bins, the judge is rewarding familiarity.
- **Same-family mitigations that remain available (the brief rules out cross-family panels):**
  - Strip any source label from judge prompts (Chae et al.).
  - Use the judge's probability for "yes", not the argmax word, so it gives a graded score.
  - Present pairs in both orders.
  - Ask checklist questions about *checkable* faults, not holistic quality. Examples: "Does any word not exist in Spanish?", "Is there a grammatical subject for the verb in line 3?", "Does line 2 contradict line 1?"
  - Use the judge to *reject the bottom tier*, never to choose among good candidates.
- **The ceiling on judge-based savings.** A rough rule from PPI's variance: with judge–human correlation ρ and many judge-scored items, the effective human sample grows by about 1/(1 − ρ²) (my calculation). That gives 1.1× at ρ = 0.3, 1.33× at 0.5, 1.73× at 0.65 and 1.96× at 0.7. Mareček's best LLM metric had ρ ≈ 0.6 with overall impression, and only 0.26 within good poems. So the 70B judge could stretch the owner's few dozen pairs by perhaps 10–70 %, not by 10×. Dorner's bound says not to expect more.
- **A small judge trained on the owner's pairs** (an Apertus-8B LoRA with a Bradley-Terry head) is the only judge route whose bias points toward the owner's taste. It needs several hundred labelled pairs (LitBench used 43k; WQRM thousands). Until then, treat it as an aim, not a tool.

### Gaps
- No study measures self-preference for same-*family* models of different sizes, such as Apertus-70B judging Apertus-8B. Goel et al. measure similarity through error overlap, not family membership.
- The sources conflict on the self-preference mechanism (perplexity vs labels). The two studies used different tasks, neither of them poetry.
- No checklist-rubric study on poetry or creative writing was found.
- No small judge has been validated on poetry in es, it, de or fr.

## 3. Measuring sense, grammar and coherence, and whether the tools hold up on verse

### Takeaway
Three families exist:
- **Acceptability classifiers** trained on CoLA-style data. They exist for es, it, de, fr and en, but are weak outside English and Italian, and they are trained on textbook sentences, not verse.
- **Reference-free "correct-and-compare" fluency scores** (Scribendi-style). Their validity is contested even for learner essays.
- **Coherence models** (shuffle tests, next-line discriminators). A fine-tuned model can saturate the global shuffle test (97.8 %) without modelling coherence, and accuracy falls sharply on harder block shuffles.

I found **no study that validates any of these tools on verse** with enjambment, hyperbaton or archaic forms. The safest validated sense signals for poems remain Mareček's two: known-word share, and an LLM semantics score that works only for rejecting bad poems.

### Cited Findings
**Acceptability (grammaticality) classifiers**
- **MELA** (ACL 2024): about 46k sentences in 10 languages, measured with MCC.
  - Per-language data: en 9,594 (CoLA), it 9,722 (ItaCoLA), es 1,088, de 1,045 and fr 1,433. The es, de and fr sets come from the Cambridge "Syntax of …" textbooks.
  - Supervised XLM-R: en 60.6, it 53.5, es 34.1, de 26.7, fr 19.0.
  - GPT-4o, 2-shot in-language: en 72.1, it 54.9, es 61.4, de 39.7, fr 37.2.
  - The best open LLM (Baichuan2, 2-shot) averaged 18.0 across languages.
  - No literary text is included. — [arXiv 2311.09033](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.09033v3); [ACL 2024.acl-long.146](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.146)
- **EsCoLA** (LREC-COLING 2024): 11,174 Spanish sentences with acceptability labels taken from reference grammars, tagged by phenomenon. Released under CC-BY 4.0 at DOI 10.34810/data1138, with baselines from four fine-tuned models and a human annotation experiment. — [ACL 2024.lrec-main.554](https://aclanthology.org/2024.lrec-main.554)

**Correct-and-compare fluency**
- **Scribendi Score.** Islam & Magnani, EMNLP 2021: a reference-free score built from GPT-2 perplexity plus token-sort and Levenshtein ratios between the original and a correction. It was reported to correlate strongly with humans. — search summary of [ACL 2021.emnlp-main.239](https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.239.pdf) (m)
- **Contested.** The unofficial implementation "was not able to fully reproduce" the correlation results. A later evaluation found the score's gaps between systems far larger than the human gaps. — [gotutiyan/scribendi_score](https://github.com/gotutiyan/scribendi_score); [arXiv 2308.08982](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.08982) (snippet only)

**Coherence models**
- **The shuffle test is saturated.** Laban, Dai, Bandarkar & Hearst (ACL 2021): fine-tuning RoBERTa on the shuffle test reaches 97.8 %, "unlikely to lead to a good model of text coherence".
  - Their proposed alternatives: a *zero-shot* shuffle test, and a *k-block shuffle test*.
  - In the k-block test, human accuracy stays around 95 % while model accuracy falls from 94 % to 78 % as block size grows. — [ACL 2021.acl-short.134](https://aclanthology.org/2021.acl-short.134/); [arXiv 2107.03448](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03448)
- **For poems, Kiasari's fine-tuned BERT** (same-poem vs other-poem negatives) produced poems humans rated more coherent. Covered in the earlier note.
- **Human coherence ratings of poems are themselves only moderately reliable.** An Arabic poetry generation study reported coherence agreement κ = 0.54. It called 0.50–0.65 the expected range for subjective judgements of creative text. — [arXiv 2604.27766](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.27766) (search snippet only)

**Verse-specific validity**
- The search found no paper pairing language-model surprisal or acceptability models with enjambed or archaic verse.
- Eye-tracking shows readers process poetry differently from prose, and enjambed lines differently from end-stopped ones. That is human data, with no model validation. — [Scientific Study of Literature 4(1), jbe-platform](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/ssol.4.1.01jag) (snippet only)
- Mareček's annotators treated formal deviations as possibly intentional, the same problem a grammar checker faces with poetic licence. — [Mareček et al. 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.eval4nlp-1.9)

### Inferences
- **Acceptability classifier for PoesIA.** For es, fine-tune an XLM-R or a multilingual encoder on EsCoLA (11k) plus ItaCoLA and CoLA. Expect MCC around 0.3–0.5 on in-domain sentences and less on verse. de and fr have only about 1k items each in MELA, so classifiers there will be weak.
  - Calibrate each classifier on human poems. Use the 5th percentile of human-poem scores as the floor, so that hyperbaton and archaism in real poems set the tolerance.
  - Score at **sentence** level (join enjambed lines up to the sentence boundary), not line level. A line cut by enjambment is not a sentence.
- **Correct-and-compare with Apertus-70B.**
  - Ask for a *minimal* correction of each sentence, then measure the character edit ratio.
  - Calibrate on poets' sentences, because the judge will "correct" licence such as word-order inversion, archaic clitics or *leísmo*.
  - Report the share of sentences needing more edits than the 95th percentile seen in human poems.
  - Treat it as a gate, never as a ranker. Validity on verse is unproven, so run it only after a check against the owner's labels.
- **Harder negatives for the sense test.** PoesIA's 90 % vs 8 % (real vs shuffled) is the easy global shuffle that Laban et al. show is saturated. Better controls:
  - (i) swap one line with a line from the same poem (local shuffle);
  - (ii) swap one line with a metrically and rhyme-matched line from another poem;
  - (iii) k-block (stanza-order) shuffles.

  A sense or coherence instrument that cannot tell (i)–(iii) from the original will not rank 8B lines against poets' lines.
- **A next-line discriminator on frozen embeddings** (the earlier note's suggestion) should be trained with these harder negatives. Report accuracy per negative type and per language, against a human-poem test split.

### Gaps
- No study validates an acceptability, GEC or coherence model on verse in any language.
- The Scribendi correlation numbers were not read in the primary paper.
- The κ = 0.54 coherence agreement for Arabic poetry is from a snippet.

## 4. Human evaluation design for poetry: pairwise, Turing-style tests, number of items and raters, experts vs lay readers, power

### Takeaway
Evidence on raters:
- Lay raters cannot replace experts for poetry. In Kaufman et al., 106 students vs 10 published poets rated 205 poems, and the mean ratings correlated only r = 0.21.
- Lay raters also cannot tell curated AI poems from human ones (Köbis & Mossink; Hitsuwari; Porter & Machery).
- **A single expert's absolute creativity rating of a poem has a reliability of only about 0.3** (Spearman-Brown, from Kaufman's α). One owner's Likert scores are therefore noisy per poem.

Relative judgements are more reliable per unit of effort. Best-worst scaling reached the same split-half reliability with 30 % of the annotations a rating scale needed. RankME raised ICC for "quality" from 0.41 to 0.68.

Typical NLP human evaluations are underpowered (Card et al.). With 40 decisive pairs, one rater detects only a preference of about 75 % or more with 80 % power.

### Cited Findings
**Expert vs lay raters**
- **Kaufman, Baer, Cole & Sexton 2008** (*Creativity Research Journal* 20(2):171–178; full text read).
  - Raters: 10 published poets and 106 college students. Each rated 205 poems for creativity on a 1–6 scale, in random order.
  - Expert–novice correlation: r(202) = 0.216, p = .003. The authors call this "far too low (4.41% shared variance)".
  - Expert α = 0.832. Student α = 0.935 over 106 raters, which falls to 0.575 when standardised to 10 raters, against 0.804 for the experts.
  - The experts were harsher: mean 3.09 vs 4.47.
  - Conclusion: replacing experts with non-experts "does not appear to be a reasonable expectation" in poetry. — [DOI 10.1080/10400410802059929](https://doi.org/10.1080/10400410802059929); [author PDF](https://www.rider.edu/sites/default/files/2024-05/CATRatersPoems.pdf)
- **Crowd workers vs teachers.** Karpinska, Akoury & Iyyer (EMNLP 2021): Mechanical Turk workers, even with strict qualifications, could not tell model-written from human-written stories, while English teachers could. Workers' ratings improved when they saw human references alongside. Most of 45 surveyed papers omitted key evaluation details. — [ACL 2021.emnlp-main.97](https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.97); [arXiv 2109.06835](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.06835)
- **Expertise changes what drives creativity ratings.** Chaudhuri et al. 2024: expertise in English literature moderated how clarity, aesthetic appeal and felt valence predicted creativity judgements of English poems (129 participants). — Goldsmiths repository, [research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/34000](https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/34000) (snippet only; journal not confirmed)

**Turing-style tests**
- **Köbis & Mossink** (*Computers in Human Behavior*):
  - Two experiments, 830 participants. GPT-2 continued human poems' first lines.
  - Participants could *not* reliably detect the AI poem when a human picked the best output (human in the loop). They *could* when a random output was used (human out of the loop).
  - Participants showed slight aversion to algorithm-generated poetry, whether or not they were told. — [arXiv 2005.09980](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.09980)
- **Hitsuwari, Ueda, Yun & Nomura** (*Computers in Human Behavior* 139, 107502; 2023):
  - 385 participants each rated 40 human-in-the-loop AI haiku, 40 out-of-the-loop AI haiku and 40 haiku by professionals.
  - Human-in-the-loop haiku scored highest on beauty. Fully AI and fully human haiku scored about the same.
  - Raters could not tell authorship, and they rated lower what they *believed* was AI-written. — [Kyoto University release](https://www.kyoto-u.ac.jp/en/research-news/2022-12-02)
- **Porter & Machery 2024** (1,634 + 696 participants): lay readers preferred ChatGPT-3.5 poems. Details are in the earlier note.

**Rating formats and reliability per unit of effort**
- **Best-worst scaling vs rating scales.** Kiritchenko & Mohammad (ACL 2017), 3,207 terms and phrases for sentiment intensity:
  - At equal annotation counts, split-half reliability was ρ = 0.98 for best-worst scaling and 0.95 for rating scales (p < .001).
  - Best-worst scaling reached ρ = 0.95 with 3N annotations, "30% of what is needed for rating scale".
  - The gap widened for linguistically complex items such as phrases with negators. — [arXiv 1712.01765](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01765)
- **RankME.** Novikova, Dušek & Rieser (NAACL 2018), crowd ratings of NLG output, 3 raters per item.
  - ICC for "quality": Likert 0.41, plain magnitude estimation 0.60, RankME 0.68.
  - Naturalness: 0.12 → 0.27 → 0.42.
  - Informativeness was already high on Likert (0.78). — [ACL N18-2012](https://aclanthology.org/N18-2012/); [arXiv 1803.05928](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.05928)

**Power**
- **Card et al., "With Little Power Comes Great Responsibility"** (EMNLP 2020), 117 Likert comparisons from 41 EMNLP 2019 papers:
  - 69 % of evaluations used ≤ 100 items and 18 % used > 200.
  - 3 annotators per item was the mode.
  - The typical design is "underpowered to detect small model differences".
  - The authors release power-analysis notebooks. — [ACL 2020.emnlp-main.745](https://aclanthology.org/2020.emnlp-main.745); [arXiv 2010.06595](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.06595)

**Form and appreciation (human experiment)**
- Rhyme and regular metre each raised aesthetic appreciation and processing intensity in stanzas modified to remove them. This sits against Mareček's finding that experts care little about form: form helps when it is present, but its absence is tolerated. — *Frontiers in Psychology* 2013, [DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00010](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00010/text) (authors from memory: Obermeier et al. (m))

### Inferences
**Single-rater reliability (my calculation from Kaufman et al.)**
- Spearman-Brown down-step: r₁ = α / (k − (k−1)α). For the experts, α = 0.832 over k = 10 gives r₁ ≈ 0.33. The standardised value 0.804 gives r₁ ≈ 0.29. For the students, α = 0.935 over k = 106 gives r₁ ≈ 0.12.
- One owner's absolute 1–6 rating of one poem therefore carries about 30 % reliable variance.
- To reach reliability 0.8 on *absolute* scores, the same poem needs about 9 independent ratings (k = 0.8·0.7 / (0.3·0.2) ≈ 9.3), and about 5 for 0.7. One owner cannot supply that, which is another reason to use pairwise or best-worst judgements and aggregate across many items.

**Power with one owner (my calculation)**
Exact two-sided binomial sign test, α = 0.05, on decisive pairs. It complements the earlier note's table, which went the other way (pairs needed for a given effect).

| Decisive pairs | Wins needed to reject 50 % | Power at true 65 % | 70 % | 75 % | 80 % |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 24 | 18 (75 %) | 0.21 | 0.39 | 0.61 | 0.81 |
| 30 | 21 (70 %) | 0.36 | 0.59 | 0.80 | 0.94 |
| 40 | 27 (67.5 %) | 0.44 | 0.70 | 0.90 | 0.98 |
| 60 | 39 (65 %) | 0.56 | 0.84 | 0.97 | 1.00 |

"A few dozen blind pairs" can therefore *decide* only large differences, about 75 % preference or more. Smaller differences need pooling across sessions or a PPI-style combination with a judge.

**Design rules for PoesIA that follow from the findings**
1. **Ask the owner relative questions.** Use pairs, or best-worst choices over 4-tuples: each 4-tuple gives 5 of the 6 implied pairwise orderings. Best-worst over 4 candidates extracts more information per judgement than one pair.
2. **Stratify pairs by the automatic score gap.** Spend most pairs where the scorecard or judge is unsure. Spend a fixed 20 % on wide-gap pairs to *validate* the automatic instruments: the owner should agree with the automatic ranking there nearly always.
3. **Combine with the judge through PPI or prediction-powered ranking.**
   - Estimate a preference rate or a rank-set from the owner's pairs plus the 70B's verdicts on many more pairs.
   - Report the interval. If the interval crosses 50 %, the honest result is "not decided".
4. **Keep lay readers out of quality decisions.** Use them only to test *audience* reception, and say so. Kaufman (r = 0.21 with experts) and Porter & Machery (lay readers prefer simpler AI verse) show that lay preference is a different target.
5. **Treat Turing-style "can you tell?" tests as a weak goal.**
   - Köbis & Mossink and Hitsuwari show that passing depends on curation (human in the loop) and on lay raters.
   - If used, run them with the owner or another expert, randomise sources, and report detection accuracy with a binomial interval.
   - 40 trials cannot separate 60 % detection from chance; detection must reach about 67.5 %.

### Gaps
- No study of test-retest reliability of a single expert rating poems was found.
- Best-worst scaling and RankME were validated on sentiment and NLG data, not poems.
- The power numbers assume independent pairs. Pairs that reuse the same poems are correlated, so true power is lower.

## 5. Multi-level coherence: development across stanzas and theme adherence in prior work

### Takeaway
Prior poetry work almost always measures coherence and theme with **one whole-poem human rating**: "is the theme of the whole quatrain consistent?" in Chinese poetry papers, and "theme alignment" in POEMetric through an LLM judge. I found no validated automatic measure of *development* or arc across stanzas for poems. The nearest validated ideas are:
- semantic-path measures for stories (speed, volume, circuitousness; covered earlier);
- reader-profile work showing that experts reward thematic development (Marco et al., covered earlier);
- a 2026 Japanese model of *incongruity resolution* in a semantic network, which predicts aesthetic appeal.

Theme adherence can be checked with a judge or with embeddings. Development needs a human-anchored definition first.

### Cited Findings
- **Chinese quatrain papers** used 1–5 human scales: Fluency ("are the lines fluent and wellformed?") and Coherence ("is the theme of the whole quatrain consistent?"). A thematic CVAE paper used "Consistency": whether the entire poem delivers a consistent theme. — [arXiv 1809.04313](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1809.04313); [arXiv 1711.07632](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.07632) (snippets only)
- **POEMetric's theme alignment** is an LLM-judge metric, validated in aggregate against 7 experts. — [arXiv 2604.03695](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.03695) (earlier note)
- **Incongruity resolution.** Kameya, Miyamoto & Utsumi (2026, *Cognitive Studies*, Japanese Cognitive Science Society) modelled poem reading as spreading activation in a semantic network. They measured activation diversity by entropy. "Incongruity resolution" had a significant positive effect on aesthetic appeal and "unresolved load" a negative one, in a linear mixed model. It works at word level and does not model stanza progression. — [J-STAGE jcss 33(3) 2026.036](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jcss/33/3/33_2026.036/_article/-char/en) (snippet only)
- **Block-order coherence is hard for models.** k-block shuffle accuracy falls to 78 % while humans stay at about 95 %. Stanza order is the poetry analogue. — [Laban et al. 2021](https://aclanthology.org/2021.acl-short.134/)

### Inferences
**A testable definition of development for PoesIA.** Development is present when:
- (a) each stanza stays near the poem's theme (theme adherence);
- (b) consecutive stanzas are not restatements (redundancy below the human 95th percentile, which PoesIA already measures);
- (c) the **stanza order matters**: the original order should be distinguishable from shuffled stanza orders.

Measure (c) with a stanza-order discriminator, Laban's k-block test with k = one stanza, trained on human poems. Its accuracy on the 8B's poems against human poems is a direct, cheap development score. For a poem whose stanzas could come in any order, the model sits at chance. That is exactly the "list of images, no arc" failure.

**Theme adherence.** Ask the judge a checkable question per stanza: "Does stanza 2 still concern X?". Use embeddings only as a pre-filter. The earlier note showed raw cosine cannot judge sense.

**Validate on human poems before use.** Before the arc score gates anything, check that it ranks the owner's blind pairs in the same direction at least as often as the scorecard does. Use the wide-gap stratum from §4 for that check.

### Gaps
- No study validates an automatic "development" or "arc" score for poems against human ratings.
- The Kameya et al. 2026 result is from a snippet, and its effect sizes were not read.
- No multi-level (line, stanza, poem) evaluation scheme for poetry with human validation at each level was found.
